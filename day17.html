UX Principles for PMs

Yesterday you learned to review bottom-up instead of reacting to whatever's most visible. Today hands you the actual toolkit for that bottom layer — five usability heuristics, translated out of design-speak and into questions you can ask in fifteen seconds, with no design degree required.

Yesterday you built the Critique Stack — Clarity, Consistency, Consequence, Craft — and learned that a review should run bottom-up, catching the load-bearing issue before anyone debates button color. What that lesson didn't give you was a concrete way to test "is this clear?" against something other than gut feel. Today's five heuristics are that test. They're the toolkit for the layer you now know to check first.
01

The Big Idea

You don't need a design degree to catch most usability problems in a B2B product — you need five questions, asked in the right order, every time. These come from Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics, first published in 1994 and still the closest thing the industry has to a shared standard for what makes an interface usable.

Thirty years on, a senior PM doesn't need all ten memorized. Five of them do almost all the work of catching the problems B2B admin tools actually ship with — the other five mostly restate ground the Critique Stack already covers, or matter more for consumer products with less forgiving users. Here's the pocket set.

1
Visibility of System Status "Does the user know what's happening right now?"

The system should always keep the user informed, through feedback that arrives within the time they expect it. A click that produces no visible response for several seconds reads as broken, even when the backend is working exactly as designed.

2
Match Between System and the Real World "Is this in the user's language, or the database's?"

Speak the user's vocabulary and follow conventions they already know from their job, not the internal field names, status codes, or data model your engineers use to think about the same thing.

3
Error Prevention "Can this mistake happen at all, or only be explained after it does?"

A well-written error message is a consolation prize. Removing the condition that causes the error — disabling an invalid option, confirming a destructive action before it runs — is worth more than explaining the mistake gracefully after the fact.

4
Recognition Rather Than Recall "Does the user have to remember something from another screen?"

Minimize how much the user has to hold in working memory. Show options, labels, and prior choices on screen instead of requiring the user to recall an ID, a setting, or a value they saw three steps ago.

5
User Control and Freedom "If the user starts this, is there a way out that isn't destructive?"

Give users a clearly marked emergency exit — undo, cancel, pause — for actions they didn't mean to take or need to back out of. Forcing a costly, irreversible path just to undo a simple choice punishes exploration.

Why these five, and not all ten

Nielsen's other five — aesthetic and minimalist design, help documentation, flexibility for expert users, error recovery, and consistency and standards — are real, but the last one is already Layer 2 of yesterday's Critique Stack, and the rest tend to matter more once a product is mature or its users are less captive than someone using the CRM your company sells them access to. These five are the ones that, left unchecked, turn into support tickets and renewal risk in enterprise software specifically.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

An ops lead at an enterprise customer builds a segment of 40 million records using nested AND/OR logic in a workflow editor, then hits a wall that a thirty-minute usability pass would have caught before it shipped.

"I clicked 'Calculate Segment Size' and nothing happened for eleven seconds, so I clicked it two more times. Then it showed me a field called 'cust_eng_score_90d_bucket' and I had no idea if that was the metric I meant. I got pulled into a call, came back twenty minutes later, and the whole thing was gone — no draft, no warning."
What the marketer experiencedHeuristic violatedThe fix
Eleven seconds of silence after clicking Calculate, leading to repeat clicksVisibility of System StatusA progress indicator or live running count, even an approximate one
Field shown as "cust_eng_score_90d_bucket" instead of a marketer-facing nameMatch to the Real World"Engagement Score (last 90 days)" — keep the raw name in a tooltip for the analysts who want it
Twenty minutes of segment logic lost with no save, no warning before navigating awayError PreventionAutosave the draft, or at minimum warn before discarding unsaved work

The reframe: none of these three required a redesign — a progress bar, a label rename, and an autosave are each less than a day of engineering work. What they required was someone walking through the flow asking the five questions before it shipped, rather than after a CSM fielded the complaint. The repeated clicks from the first violation are also a quieter risk: three clicks on "Calculate" against a 40-million-row query can be three queued jobs, not one — the kind of cost nobody notices until the infra bill does.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation A junior PM sends you a mock for "Bulk Segment Merge" the night before design review — a tool that lets a marketer combine two existing segments (say, 2 million and 800,000 records) into one new segment. The mock shows two dropdowns listing segment IDs ("seg_44291", "seg_50103"), a "Merge" button, and a success toast once it's done. The merge can't be undone once it runs. The junior PM asks: what should I flag before tomorrow's review?

Run the five heuristics against the mock

  • Match to the real world: the dropdowns show raw IDs like "seg_44291," not segment names. Nobody building a campaign thinks in segment IDs — they think "VIP churners, Q3" and "lapsed trial users." Fix this before anything else; it's the one thing that makes the whole screen unreadable.
  • Visibility of system status: merging 2.8 million records isn't instant. If the mock jumps straight from click to success toast with nothing in between, a marketer who clicks twice because nothing seemed to happen can trigger the merge job twice.
  • Error prevention: this is the one that should stop the review cold. The merge is irreversible and the mock has no confirmation step, no preview of the resulting segment size, no dry run. An accidental merge of the wrong two segments is not a five-minute fix — it's a support escalation and a trust problem.
  • User control and freedom: directly downstream of the error-prevention gap — once the merge completes, there's no undo. At minimum this needs a confirm-with-preview step; ideally, a brief undo window after the merge, the way an email client lets you unsend.
  • Recognition over recall: once names replace IDs, this mostly resolves itself — the marketer can recognize the right segments in the dropdown instead of having to recall which ID was which.

What you tell the junior PM before the meeting

  • "Don't lead with the ID-versus-name issue even though it's the most visible — lead with: this merge is irreversible and there's no confirmation step. Ask the room directly: what happens if someone merges the wrong two segments, and how do they get back to where they were? If the answer is 'they can't,' that's not a review note, that's a blocker."

The trap this drill is testing

  • The mock looks finished — two dropdowns and a button is about as simple as a UI gets. Simple screens are exactly where error prevention gets skipped, because there's no visual complexity to prompt the question. The fewer the elements, the easier it is to forget that one of them is a one-way door.
MEETING MOVE

Use this in any design or spec review

One question that surfaces most of the five heuristics at once, before the room gets pulled into visual feedback:

"Walk me through what the user sees while this is running, and what their way out is if they need to undo it."
05

Glossary

Nielsen's Usability Heuristics
Ten general principles for interaction design, published by Jakob Nielsen in 1994 and still the industry's most widely used shared standard for evaluating an interface without a formal usability study.
Visibility of System Status
The principle that a system should keep users informed about what's happening through timely, appropriate feedback — a spinner, a progress bar, a running count — rather than leaving them guessing after an action.
Recognition Rather Than Recall
Minimizing a user's memory load by making objects, actions, and options visible, instead of requiring them to remember information from an earlier screen or step.
Error Prevention
Designing so a mistake can't be made in the first place — disabling invalid choices, confirming destructive actions — rather than relying on a well-worded error message after the fact.
Progressive Disclosure
Showing only what a user needs for the step they're on, and revealing advanced or secondary options only when asked for — a way to serve both new and expert users without overwhelming either.
