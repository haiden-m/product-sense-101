Data-Informed vs Data-Driven

Yesterday's five heuristics catch problems hiding in a screen. Today's distinction catches problems hiding in a dashboard — because a number can be completely accurate and still point you at the wrong decision.

Yesterday you got five questions for reading a screen — whether the user knows what's happening, whether the mistake could've been prevented, whether there's a way out. Today hands you the same kind of discipline for reading a dashboard. A metric is just as capable of hiding the real story as a confusing UI is — it's just better dressed, because it arrives looking like proof instead of a guess.
01

The Big Idea

Data-driven means the metric is the decision: a number moves, an action follows, no further questions asked. Data-informed means the metric is one input among several — alongside qualitative research, business context, and judgment about what the number can't see.

The two get confused because both sound rigorous and both involve the same dashboards. The difference shows up at the moment a number comes back ambiguous, thin, or surprising. A data-driven read stops at the headline figure and acts on it. A data-informed read treats the headline figure as the start of a question: what segment is this an average of, what's the mechanism behind it, and who would tell us something different if we asked them directly.

— Data-Driven "The metric moved. Act on the metric."

The number is treated as self-explanatory. No segment cut, no customer conversation, no causal story required before acting — the dashboard is the decision-maker.

Fits: high-volume, narrow, reversible calls with a stable, well-understood causal link — a pricing-page headline, a checkout button copy test.

+ Data-Informed "The metric moved. What's the question it's actually answering?"

Data narrows where to look and what to investigate; judgment, segmentation, and direct customer contact decide what to do about it. Slower, and worth it when being wrong is expensive.

Fits: strategic bets, sunset-or-keep calls, anything where the "why" changes the right action — not just the next test.

This is the one-way/two-way door test again

Day 14's distinction maps onto this one almost exactly. A two-way door — reversible, cheap to undo, narrow blast radius — is a fine candidate for pure data-driven calls: run the test, read the number, ship the winner. A one-way door — a feature sunset, a default that applies to every customer, anything expensive or embarrassing to reverse — needs the data-informed treatment, because the cost of acting on a number that was hiding the real story is exactly the cost you can't walk back.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

Six months after launch, your analytics product's "Predictive Audience Builder" — an AI feature that auto-builds high-propensity segments — sits at 11% overall adoption. Leadership, reading only that headline number, proposes sunsetting it next quarter to free up the team for other work. Before agreeing, you pull one more cut: adoption and revenue contribution by account tier.

Account tierWeekly adoptionShare of total ARRWhat the aggregate hid
Enterprise (top 100)58%70%It's a core retention driver for your highest-value cohort, invisible inside an 11% average
Mid-market9%24%Low usage, but CS calls trace it to the feature being buried three clicks deep — a discoverability gap, not a desirability one
SMB / long-tail3%6%Genuinely low relevance here — these accounts don't run complex enough segmentation to need it

The reframe: "11% adoption, cut it" is a data-driven read of a number that was quietly averaging together a feature's biggest success and its real discoverability problem. The data-informed version keeps the feature — your top accounts anchor 70% of ARR around it — and redirects the team to the actual issue: a navigation and naming fix for mid-market, not a build-vs-kill decision at all.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation Your dashboard shows that only 6% of customers have turned on "Auto-Approve AI-Generated Subject Lines" since launch two months ago. Your CPO, prepping for the board deck due tomorrow, wants to cite this as proof the AI feature set is underwhelming and deprioritize the next three AI features on the roadmap. You have one day. What do you check, and what do you tell her?

What a single snapshot number can't tell you

  • Check the trend, not the point-in-time snapshot. Is 6% flat across every customer's tenure, or is it climbing fastest among customers onboarded in the last 30 days? An opt-in trust feature can take two to three quarters to ramp — a two-month-old adoption curve read as a verdict is a data-driven mistake dressed up as rigor.
  • Check what "adoption" is actually standing in for. The question that matters for the board isn't "did they turn it on," it's "does it work when they do." Pull output quality or time-saved for the 6% who use it. Low adoption with high value-when-used is an awareness or trust problem, not a product-market-fit problem — and those get fixed with very different roadmap decisions.
  • Talk to CS and sales before the deck is final. The dashboard can't tell you whether the real blocker is "marketers don't want AI writing subject lines they can't review first" — a specific, fixable trust objection you'll only hear in a conversation, never in an adoption percentage.

What you tell the CPO before the board meeting

  • "Don't cite 6% adoption as evidence the AI bet failed — it conflates an early-stage, opt-in trust feature with a verdict on the whole category. If we present it as failure, we risk killing investment in a wedge that typically takes longer than two months to earn trust. Let's show the adoption trend plus one CS quote on the actual objection, instead of the single snapshot number."

The trap this drill is testing

  • A single, true number is exactly what makes a board deck feel decisive — it's quotable, it's clean, and it requires no further explanation. None of that makes it the right number to act on. The simplicity that makes a stat board-ready is unrelated to whether it's telling you the real story.
MEETING MOVE

Use this whenever someone presents a dashboard as a decision

One question that forces the segment cut and the qualitative check before anyone commits to acting:

"Before we act on this number — what segment is it an average of, and who in it would tell us something different if we asked directly?"
05

Glossary

Data-Driven
Decision-making where a metric's movement directly dictates the action, with no further qualitative check — appropriate for narrow, reversible, high-volume calls with a stable causal link.
Data-Informed
Decision-making where data narrows the hypothesis space and judgment, segmentation, and qualitative research decide the action — the right default for strategic or hard-to-reverse calls.
Goodhart's Law
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Describes how a metric gets gamed or distorted the moment people are rewarded for moving it directly, rather than for the outcome it was meant to stand in for.
Simpson's Paradox
A trend that holds in several individual segments reverses or disappears once those segments are combined into one aggregate figure — a core reason averages can mislead even when every underlying number is accurate.
Survivorship Bias
Drawing conclusions only from the subset that stuck around — current users, completed signups, retained accounts — while ignoring the ones who dropped out or never engaged, which skews the picture toward whoever is still in the dataset.
