One-Way vs Two-Way Doors

Yesterday sorted features by the shape of satisfaction they produce. Today sorts decisions by a question that has nothing to do with how big they feel in the room: if this turns out wrong, how expensive is it to walk back?

Yesterday the Kano Model separated features by curve shape — must-be, performance, attractive — so you'd stop scoring all of them as if "more investment" meant "more satisfaction." Today is a second, independent axis that sits on top of any framework you've already used to decide what to build: once you've picked the feature, how much process does the decision to ship it actually deserve? A delighter and a must-be can both be two-way doors. A RICE score of 9 and a RICE score of 3 can both be one-way doors. Reversibility doesn't care what framework put the item on your roadmap.
01

The Big Idea

Jeff Bezos's 1997 shareholder letter drew a line that most product orgs still get backwards: some decisions are one-way doors, and some are two-way doors, and the entire discipline is matching the weight of your process to which kind you're walking through.

A two-way door is a decision you can reverse quickly and cheaply if it turns out wrong. Change the default send frequency for one segment, tweak an onboarding flow's copy, A/B test a new pricing page layout — walk through, look around, and if it's bad, walk back out. Almost nothing is lost by moving fast, because the cost of being wrong is small and temporary.

A one-way door is a decision that's expensive, slow, or impossible to undo once you're through it. Migrating customer data to a new schema, deprecating an API that partners have built integrations against, signing a multi-year contract with a payments processor, telling a customer their data retention policy changed. Walking back out costs real money, real trust, or isn't physically possible at all.

Two-way door (Type 2)

Reversible at low cost. Decide with incomplete information, using a small group, fast. Most decisions — even ones that feel big in the meeting — are actually this. A feature flag, an opt-in beta, a change scoped to one customer segment are all built-in reverse gears.

One-way door (Type 1)

Irreversible, or reversible only at high cost in money, time, or trust. Decide deliberately, with broader input and real data, slowly. Schema migrations, breaking API changes already adopted downstream, and anything that changes what customers were promised fall here.

The mistake runs in both directions

Treat a two-way door like a one-way door and you get decision debt: committee reviews, six-week sign-off chains, and a roadmap that moves at the speed of its slowest approver — for a change you could've shipped behind a flag and learned from in a day. Treat a one-way door like a two-way door and you get the opposite failure: a schema change pushed on Friday because "we can always roll back," except the migration already ran against production data and rolling back means a data-loss incident, not a revert commit. The skill isn't speed and it isn't caution — it's correctly sorting which door you're at before you decide how carefully to open it.

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

A B2B SaaS team ships two changes in the same sprint, and the team applies exactly the wrong amount of process to each.

"Smart Send Time needs six weeks of review before GA — it touches deliverability, and deliverability is scary. The webhook payload change is just adding a field, we'll ship it Thursday."
ChangeIf it's wrongCost to reverseActual door
Smart Send Time (AI-picked send hour, opt-in beta, flag-gated)Some sends land at a worse hour for some customersFlip the flag off; affected sends resume old schedule next cycleTwo-way
Webhook payload adds a required field, no deprecation windowPartner integrations parsing strict schemas start failing silentlyPartners have already shipped code against it; fixing means a second breaking change plus support firesOne-way

The reframe: "touches deliverability" made Smart Send Time feel dangerous, but it's gated behind a flag and scoped to opted-in beta customers — the reverse gear was built in from day one, so a six-week review board mostly just burns six weeks. The webhook field felt small because it's "just one field," but a webhook contract is a promise to code you don't control and can't instantly update — the moment a partner's production system parses it, you're through a door that locks behind you. Feeling big and being irreversible aren't the same property, and this team scored by the first one instead of the second.

Scenario Drill

The situation Finance wants to change how multi-touch campaign attribution windows work — from a 7-day lookback to a 30-day lookback — to better match the sales cycle customers report. Growth is pushing to ship it to every account by Friday: "it's just a config change, we can always flip it back." But the attribution window doesn't just drive a live dashboard — it's the basis for historical reports customers have already exported to their own BI tools, and several enterprise accounts tie internal commission payouts to the attribution numbers your platform reports each quarter. Is this a one-way door or a two-way door, and what do you tell Growth?

Step 1 — Separate the config from the consequence

  • Growth is right that flipping a lookback-window setting is technically trivial — that part really is a two-way door. But the decision being evaluated isn't "can we change a config value," it's "can we change what a number has meant to every downstream system and human that already relied on the old meaning." Those are different doors, and the easy one is masking the hard one.

Step 2 — Trace who already treated the old number as ground truth

  • Customers exported historical reports under the 7-day definition and built their own calculations on top of it. Commission payouts were already calculated and, in some cases, already paid using that definition. Reverting the config next week doesn't un-pay a commission or un-export a spreadsheet — the moment the new numbers touch a customer's downstream process, you're through the door, flag or no flag.

Step 3 — Reframe the decision for Growth

  • "Technically this is a one-line config change, but it's a one-way door for anyone who already built on the old definition — which includes commission calculations we can't retroactively fix with an apology. I don't want to block the idea; I want to scope it as a new attribution model customers opt into going forward, with the 7-day number preserved and clearly labeled for anything already in flight, instead of silently redefining what 'attribution' has meant in every report that already shipped."

The trap this drill is testing

  • "We can always flip it back" is true of the switch and false of everything the switch touches. The senior move is asking not "can we technically reverse this setting" but "can we reverse every consequence that will have already happened by the time we notice it was wrong" — and sizing the process to that answer, not the first one.
MEETING MOVE

Use this before a decision gets its process level set

Next time a decision is about to get six weeks of review, or none at all, redirect with:

"If this turns out wrong, what does it actually cost to walk back — and who's already acted on it by the time we find out? Is this a door we can step back through, or one that locks behind us?"
05

Glossary

One-Way Door (Type 1 Decision)
A decision that's irreversible, or reversible only at high cost in money, time, or trust. Warrants slower deliberation, wider input, and real data before committing.
Two-Way Door (Type 2 Decision)
A decision that can be reversed quickly and cheaply if it's wrong. Should be made fast, by a small group, with incomplete information — the reverse gear is the safety net.
Reversibility Cost
What it actually takes to undo a decision once made — not just the technical effort, but the money, time, and trust spent walking it back, including consequences others have already acted on.
Decision Velocity
How fast an organization can make and act on decisions. Usually throttled not by genuinely hard choices but by applying one-way-door process to decisions that were two-way doors all along.
Blast Radius
The scope of who and what is affected if a decision turns out wrong — how many customers, systems, or downstream processes are exposed. A key input into whether a door is actually reversible in practice.
