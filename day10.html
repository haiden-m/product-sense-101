Product–Market Fit

The one state every strategic framework this week has been pointing toward — and the one most B2B SaaS teams misdiagnose, in both directions.

Yesterday you mapped platform thinking and network effects — how a product's value can compound as usage grows. Today closes the week by asking the harder question underneath all of it: compounds for whom, and how would you actually know? PMF is the checkpoint that tells you whether the moat, the segment, and the wedge you've been analyzing all week are even worth defending yet.
01

The Big Idea

Product–market fit is not a feeling. It's the point where a defined segment pulls your product forward faster than your sales and marketing can push it — where retention, expansion, and referral start doing structural work that acquisition spend used to do alone.

Marc Andreessen's original formulation was blunt: "You can always feel product–market fit when it's happening." That line gets quoted constantly and misapplied just as often, because it describes the symptom, not the test. Senior PMs don't wait to feel it. They instrument for it, because B2B SaaS has a specific failure mode: you can mistake a motivated champion, a generous pilot budget, or a single whale logo for market pull, when what you actually have is one person's enthusiasm propping up a product nobody else asked for.

The useful reframe: PMF isn't a company-wide state, it's segment-specific. One product has PMF with enterprise retail ops teams running coordinated multi-channel programs. That tells you nothing about whether the same product has PMF with a mid-market subscription business that wants onboarding automation first and lifecycle orchestration second. A platform can be in fit with one ICP and pre-fit with the adjacent one it's trying to expand into — which is exactly the situation most "why isn't this new segment adopting like our core base did" conversations are actually about.

Why this is hard to self-diagnose

Every team has evidence it can point to for PMF — a few happy logos, inbound interest, a press mention. The discipline is in weighting the right evidence: retention curves that flatten instead of decay to zero, usage that deepens without hand-holding, expansion revenue that outpaces new-logo revenue, and prospects who would be genuinely upset if the product disappeared. Anecdote is cheap. A flattening cohort curve is not.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

A B2B SaaS product ships a new "Workflow Automation" module 14 months ago. Adoption looks strong on paper: 40 of 220 eligible accounts have turned it on. Leadership wants to know — is this PMF, or not yet?

"We have 40 logos using it. Isn't that proof the market wants this?"

The senior PM's actual read, framework-first:

Sean Ellis Test, run on those 40 accounts: "How would you feel if you could no longer use Workflow Automation?"

Very disappointed
22%
Somewhat disappointed
31%
Not disappointed
47%

The 40%-"very disappointed" threshold Ellis popularized is a rule of thumb, not a law — but at 22%, this is nowhere close. Cross-referenced against usage data: of the 40 accounts, 11 were pushed in by their CSM to hit an adoption KPI and have logged in twice since onboarding. That's not pull, that's push wearing pull's clothing.

The actual diagnosis: This isn't failed PMF — it's undiagnosed segmentation. The 9 accounts that scored "very disappointed" share a profile: mid-market retail and travel brands running 15+ concurrent campaigns who were manually stitching journey logic in spreadsheets before. For that segment, early signal looks real. For the other 31, the module solved a problem they didn't have urgently enough to resist being unseated by the next roadmap item. The move isn't "kill it" or "scale it" — it's narrow the ICP, re-run the test on a cleaner segment, and stop counting logo-count as the metric that matters.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation Your B2B SaaS product, 8 months post-launch of a real-time insights add-on sold to existing customers. 18 accounts have adopted it. Your VP of Product asks you in the hallway: "Do we have PMF on the add-on yet? GTM wants to put it in the keynote next quarter." You have three days before you need an answer. What do you actually do?

Step 1 — Refuse the binary

  • "Do we have PMF" is a yes/no question about something that is segment-specific and graduated. Reframe the ask: "Let me tell you which segments show fit, which show early signal, and which show none — and what that means for a keynote claim."

Step 2 — Pull the right evidence in 3 days

  • Retention/usage curve on the 18 accounts by cohort month — flattening (good) vs. decaying to zero (bad). This is available same-day from product analytics; don't wait on a survey.
  • Sean Ellis pulse — a 5-minute async survey to the 18 primary admins, even with a small N. Directional signal beats no signal.
  • Expansion vs. push — how many of the 18 were self-initiated trials vs. CSM-driven pilots with incentives attached. This single cut usually explains half the adoption number.
  • Segment the 18 by firmographic and use-case — industry vertical, campaign volume, prior workaround. Look for a cluster, not an average.

Step 3 — Answer the VP with a segmented verdict, not a number

  • Something like: "Strong early-fit signal with high-volume retail accounts running 10+ campaigns — 6 of 18, all self-initiated, usage climbing month over month. Weak or no signal elsewhere. I'd recommend the keynote lead with that specific segment's story, not a company-wide PMF claim — overclaiming here sets a renewal and expansion narrative we can't back up a year from now."

The trap this drill is testing

  • The failure mode isn't picking the wrong framework — it's answering the binary question as asked, under time pressure, with a vanity metric (logo count, feature flag enabled) because it's the fastest number to produce. The senior move is spending one of your three days re-scoping the question itself.
MEETING MOVE

Use this in your next roadmap or QBR conversation

Next time someone cites adoption count as proof of fit, redirect with:

"Which segment is pulling this hardest, and what would they lose if we took it away — versus who adopted it because we asked them to?"
05

Glossary

Product–Market Fit (PMF)
The state in which a well-defined customer segment experiences enough value from a product that retention, usage depth, and organic referral become self-sustaining — distinct from early traction, which can be driven entirely by sales push or incentive.
Sean Ellis Test
A survey asking users "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" The 40%-"very disappointed" threshold is a popularized heuristic for fit, not a certified benchmark — useful as a directional signal, dangerous as the only input to a go/no-go call.
Cohort Retention Curve
Usage or revenue retention plotted by signup cohort over time. A curve that flattens above zero (an "asymptote") signals a durable core of engaged users; one that decays toward zero signals the product hasn't found its segment yet, regardless of top-line growth.
Push vs. Pull Adoption
Push adoption is driven by sales incentive, CSM pressure, or contractual bundling; pull adoption is customer-initiated, driven by the product solving a problem they were already actively trying to solve. Only pull is reliable PMF evidence.
Segment-Level Fit
The principle that PMF is never company-wide — a platform can be in fit with one ICP (e.g., enterprise retail) while being pre-fit with an adjacent one it's expanding into (e.g., mid-market subscription businesses), and treating the two as one number produces false confidence or false alarm.
