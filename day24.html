Writing Crisp PRDs

Yesterday you learned to name a single Accountable owner so a decision has somewhere to land. Today's lesson is the document you hand that owner — one built to get a yes or no in one meeting, not one that quietly reopens the scope fight you already won.

Yesterday you learned that most stalled launches don't fail from a bad plan — they fail because no single person was named Accountable for the call. Today addresses what you put in front of that person. A RACI chart tells the room who decides. A crisp PRD is what lets them actually decide fast, instead of re-litigating scope across six Slack threads before they can.
01

The Big Idea

A PRD has exactly one job: let someone who wasn't in any of your discovery conversations — an engineer joining the project, a VP seeing it for the first time, the Accountable owner from yesterday's lesson — read it and know precisely what's being built, why, and how you'll know it worked, without you in the room to explain it. Most PRDs fail that test not because they're too short, but because they're too long: every stakeholder's request gets added rather than triaged, until the document is a requirements warehouse instead of a decision.

The fix isn't a template. It's accepting that a crisp PRD commits to two things equally: what you're building, and what you deliberately aren't. A problem statement names one customer and one measurable pain. A success metric is a number with a target and a date, falsifiable before launch rather than describable after it. And the section most PRDs skip — Non-Goals — is the one doing the real work: it's where scope fights get settled in writing, before engineering starts, instead of negotiated live in a sprint planning meeting where nobody can see the whole tradeoff at once.

Problem Statement Who hurts, and how badly

One paragraph: the customer, the pain, and the evidence it's real — a number, a ticket volume, a churn rate. Not "customers want better X."

Success Metric How you'll know it worked

A single number that moves, with a target and a date. "Improve the experience" can't be wrong, which is exactly why it convinces no one.

Non-Goals What this deliberately won't do

The scope fight happens here, in writing, before the sprint starts — not three weeks in, after half the team already assumed otherwise.

Open Questions What's unresolved, and who owns it

Named, assigned, and dated — never a bare "TBD" that quietly becomes nobody's job and everybody's assumption.

  • A PRD is a decision document, not a requirements warehouse. Its job is to get the Accountable owner from Day 23 to a yes or no — not to catalog every idea anyone raised during discovery.
  • Non-Goals do more work than Goals. A sharp Non-Goals section is what stops a frequency-capping feature from quietly growing into a full campaign-orchestration rebuild six weeks into the build.
  • A success metric has to be falsifiable before it ships, not describable after. "Cut duplicate-send tickets by 40% in 60 days" is a bet you can be wrong about. "Improve the customer experience" is a bet you can't lose — which is exactly why it decides nothing.
  • Open Questions aren't an admission of weakness. Listing what you genuinely don't know yet, with a named owner and a date to resolve it, is what separates a PRD a VP will sign off on from one that just looks finished.
— The Everything PRD Fourteen pages: frequency capping, send-time A/B testing, a new audience builder, and a reporting redesign — because every stakeholder's ask got added instead of triaged.

No single decision is being asked for, so there's nothing a VP can approve in one sitting. Each new request quietly expands scope instead of getting routed to its own, later PRD.

What happens: the doc sits unsigned for a month while engineering tries to estimate five projects as one.

+ The One-Decision PRD Frequency capping only, with the other four ideas named and explicitly parked under Non-Goals for separate, future PRDs.

One scoped decision, one owner who can actually approve it, one metric that proves it worked. The parked ideas aren't lost — they're next quarter's backlog instead of this quarter's scope creep.

What happens: engineering scopes it in a day, and the Accountable owner signs off in the meeting it's first presented.

This is where yesterday's name gets a document to act on

RACI gives you a single Accountable person empowered to say yes. A crisp PRD is the only artifact that lets them actually do it in one sitting — because the problem, the metric, and the boundaries are already decided, and all that's left is the call.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

Your team is building cross-channel frequency capping — a single view of how many messages a customer gets across email, SMS, and push, so VIP segments stop getting buried. The first draft PRD took five weeks of revisions and still hadn't been signed. Here's what changed between that draft and the one that got approved in one meeting.

PRD sectionBloated first draftCrisp revision
Problem statement"Customers want better control over messaging frequency""Customers running 3+ active campaigns can't see combined send volume per recipient — VIP segments get 11+ messages/week across channels and churn at 2.3× the rate of lower-volume segments (support data, Q3)"
Success metric"Improve customer satisfaction with messaging cadence""Cut the share of customers receiving 8+ cross-channel messages/week from 14% to under 5%, measured 60 days post-launch"
Non-GoalsNot written — every related idea stayed "in scope for now"Per-channel capping (exists already), AI-recommended optimal frequency, and capping on transactional or triggered sends are explicitly out
Open questionsBuried across 40 Slack messages in three different channels"Does capping apply retroactively to already-scheduled sends? Owner: eng lead, due before Thursday's sprint planning"
Time to sign-off5 weeks of revisions1 meeting

The reframe: the underlying feature never changed. What changed is that the PRD stopped trying to justify four adjacent ideas at once and committed, in writing, to exactly one problem, one metric, and three explicit Non-Goals — which is what let the Accountable owner actually read it and decide.

Scenario Drill

The situation You're writing the PRD for "Smart Suppression Lists" — a feature that recalculates who should not receive a campaign, based on recent engagement. Leadership wants it live in six weeks to honor a renewal commitment made to one customer. In discovery, you've heard:
  • Sales wants per-channel suppression rules (email vs. SMS vs. push).
  • Marketing wants a UI letting them manually override the algorithm per campaign.
  • Your eng lead suggests integrating it with the frequency-capping feature from Day 1 of the roadmap.
  • Nobody can say who decided it, but everyone's been assuming real-time, sub-second recalculation rather than a nightly batch job.
Write the opening problem statement and the Non-Goals section you'd put in this PRD, and name the one open question you'd flag before anyone starts building.

The assignment to avoid

  • Writing a problem statement broad enough to justify all four asks at once. Once that happens, nothing in it is falsifiable, and the Non-Goals section ends up empty — because everything stayed "maybe in scope" rather than getting a real decision.

Problem statement that matches the real deadline

  • "[Renewal customer]'s contract renews in six weeks contingent on suppressing stale leads from campaigns; today's suppression logic only checks list membership, not recent engagement, so 18% of this customer's sends go to contacts who haven't engaged in 90+ days."

Non-Goals for this release

  • Per-channel suppression rules — parked for a v2 PRD once the core engagement model ships.
  • Manual per-campaign override UI — parked; the algorithm can run without it, and it doesn't block the renewal.
  • Integration with frequency capping — noted as a dependency to monitor, not a requirement for this release.

The one open question to flag

  • Real-time vs. nightly batch recalculation. This is the one assumption that actually changes the architecture — it needs a named owner and a date to resolve before a single line of code gets written, not a discovery made mid-sprint when the batch job turns out to be half-built.
"This PRD covers engagement-based suppression only, scoped to this renewal's six-week deadline. Per-channel rules and manual overrides are explicitly out — they don't block the commitment and can ship as a v2. One thing has to be settled before sprint planning: real-time or nightly recalculation. Eng lead owns an answer by Thursday, because it changes which architecture we build."
MEETING MOVE

Use this before a PRD goes out for review

Ask it of yourself, and then of the room, before the doc is called final:

"If this PRD only had to get a yes or no on one thing, what would that one thing be — and can everything else move to Non-Goals?"
05

Glossary

PRD (Product Requirements Document)
A decision document, not a requirements catalog — its job is to let an Accountable owner approve a single scoped change, and let an engineer build it correctly without you in the room.
Non-Goals
What a release deliberately won't include, named explicitly so scope fights get resolved in writing before a sprint starts instead of negotiated mid-build.
Success Metric
A single measurable number with a target and a date, falsifiable before launch. "Improve the experience" isn't one, because it can't be proven wrong.
Open Questions
Genuinely unresolved decisions listed with a named owner and a due date — distinct from a vague "TBD" that quietly becomes an unowned assumption.
Scope Creep
The gradual, unauthorized growth of a PRD as adjacent requests get folded in rather than triaged into their own future document — the exact failure Non-Goals exists to prevent.
