Week 1 · The PM Mental Model

First Principles Thinking

Stop borrowing other people's assumptions. Learn to reason from ground truth — the skill that separates PMs who copy features from PMs who create categories.

The Big Idea

First principles thinking is the practice of breaking a problem down to its most fundamental truths — the things you know to be true independent of convention, precedent, or what competitors do — and reasoning up from there.

Most PMs reason by analogy: "Salesforce does X, so we should do X." "Our competitor launched Y, so we need Y." Analogy is fast, but it's fragile — it inherits someone else's assumptions without examining whether they apply to your context, your users, or your market position.

First principles asks a harder question: "What do we know to be true about our users, our market, and our technology — and what can we build from those truths alone?"

Analogy
"Every marketing platform has a drag-and-drop campaign builder. We need one too."
First Principles
"Our users need to define segments, select channels, set timing, and compose messages. What's the fastest path from intent to execution for our users?"

The first principles version might still lead to a drag-and-drop builder — but it might also lead to a template library with smart defaults, or an AI flow that asks three questions and generates the campaign. You don't know until you reason from the ground up.

A Practical Tool: The Five Whys

First principles can feel abstract. The Five Whys makes it concrete. Keep asking "Why?" until you hit bedrock — the fundamental truth or constraint that can't be decomposed further.

Surface "We need to add a new reporting dashboard."
Why? "Because customers keep asking for more analytics."
Why? "Because they can't answer basic questions about campaign performance."
Why? "Because our current reports show vanity metrics, not actionable insights."
Root Reports were designed around what was easy to compute, not what marketers need to decide.

Now you're at the real problem. The solution isn't "more dashboards" — it's redesigning existing reports around decision moments. That's a fundamentally different product decision, and you only get there by pushing past the surface request.

The B2B SaaS Angle

B2B SaaS is especially prone to analogy reasoning, for four structural reasons:

The Feature Parity Trap. Competitors are visible (their feature pages, G2 comparisons). Enterprise buyers create feature checklists that force convergence. Sales teams relay "they have X, why don't we?" constantly. Category analysts define expected capabilities. All of this pressure pushes you toward copying — and away from first-principles differentiation.

First principles thinking doesn't mean ignoring competitors. It means understanding why a feature exists before copying what it is. The competitor's context — their user base, their technical architecture, their market position — is almost certainly different from yours.

Worked Example

The Pricing Page Redesign

Your SaaS company is redesigning pricing. The team's instinct: study competitors' pricing pages, adopt the standard three-tier layout with feature comparison table and "Contact Sales" for enterprise.

A first principles approach decomposes differently:

Analogy Questions
What do competitors charge? What checkout flow does Company X use? How many tiers are "standard"?
First Principles Questions
What are the fundamental value drivers for our customers? What's their actual buying process? What model aligns our revenue with the value we create?

This might lead you to usage-based pricing instead of seat-based. Or a two-tier model instead of three. Or a "start free, pay when you see ROI" structure that your competitors can't match because their cost structure is different. The answer comes from your fundamentals, not the industry template.

Scenario Drill

Your CEO returns from a conference and says: "Every major player in our space is building an AI copilot. We need one too — make it our Q1 priority."

How would you apply first principles thinking to this directive? Consider:

  • What fundamental problem would an AI copilot solve for your specific users?
  • Is there evidence your users need this, or is this competitive anxiety?
  • What first-principles questions would you ask before committing 3 months of engineering?
  • How do you push back constructively without dismissing the CEO's input?
The hard part isn't the framework — it's using it diplomatically with a senior stakeholder.

Don't dismiss the CEO. That's not first principles — that's insubordination dressed as rigor. Instead, decompose the request:

Start with the user: What repetitive, high-effort tasks do your users perform today? Where do they get stuck? "AI copilot" is a solution — what's the problem it maps to in your specific product?

Examine the assumption: "Every major player is building one" — are they building the same thing? Probably not. Some are doing AI analytics, some content generation, some workflow automation. The category "AI copilot" is too vague to be a spec.

Find the first principle: Your users are marketers who spend 60% of their time on campaign setup and 40% on analysis. If AI compresses setup time, that's one value proposition. If it surfaces insights they'd otherwise miss, that's a different one. Pick based on evidence about your users, not conference buzz.

Propose a scoped experiment: "Let's spend 2 weeks validating which AI use case our top 10 customers would actually pay more for, then scope Q1 around the winner." This honors the CEO's instinct (AI matters) while applying first principles (but which AI, and for whom?).

Meeting Move of the Day

When someone proposes copying a competitor's feature, ask:

"What problem did they build that to solve — and do we have the same problem?"

This isn't confrontational. It honors the observation (the competitor did build it) while redirecting the conversation to first principles. It also subtly teaches the room that features exist to solve problems, not to match checklists.

Glossary
First Principles Thinking
Reasoning from fundamental truths rather than by analogy, convention, or precedent. Decompose a problem to its base elements and reconstruct from there.
Reasoning by Analogy
Making decisions based on what others have done, assuming their context matches yours. Fast but fragile — it inherits hidden assumptions without examination.
Five Whys
A root-cause analysis technique where you ask "Why?" iteratively to move from surface symptoms to underlying causes. Originated in Toyota's manufacturing system, now a staple PM tool.
Feature Parity Trap
The compulsion to match every competitor feature, leading to a bloated product that does many things adequately rather than a few things exceptionally. Driven by sales pressure and buyer checklists.
Convergent Design
When products in a category become increasingly similar because everyone copies each other rather than innovating from user needs. The end state of widespread analogy reasoning.
