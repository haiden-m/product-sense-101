What Is Product Sense?

The intuition that separates good PMs from great ones — and why your technical background is both your advantage and your blind spot.

The Big Idea

Product sense is the ability to make good product decisions without complete data. It's the intuition that lets you look at a feature, a user flow, or a market signal and know whether something is right or off.

But here's what most people miss: product sense isn't magical intuition. It's pattern recognition built from four distinct lenses — and you can train each one deliberately.

User Lens
Context, motivations, pain. Not what users say — what they're trying to accomplish.
Business Lens
Strategy, unit economics, competitive position. A loved feature that destroys margins isn't product sense.
Technical Lens
Feasibility, cost, leverage. What creates compounding value at the architecture level.
Market Lens
Trends, competitive moves, timing. Is the window opening or closing?

Product sense is the ability to hold all four lenses simultaneously and make trade-offs between them. Every strong product decision weighs all four. Every weak one over-indexes on just one.

The Technical PM Trap. When you drift toward product engineering, the Technical Lens hypertrophies. You start evaluating features by what's elegant to build rather than what's valuable to ship. You solve problems by reaching for architecture rather than reaching for the customer. The other three lenses atrophy. The fix isn't to suppress your technical strength — it's to deliberately exercise the other three until they're equally strong.
The B2B SaaS Angle

In B2B SaaS — especially B2B products with deep daily workflows — product sense has an extra dimension. You're building for power users with strong opinions, whose requests frequently conflate symptoms with root causes.

A customer says "I need a button that does X." Product sense is hearing that and asking "What outcome are you trying to achieve that made you think of X?"

And in enterprise, you have a split audience: the user (the practitioner working in the product daily) and the buyer (the VP who signs the renewal). Their jobs-to-be-done are often different. Strong B2B product sense means satisfying both without letting either dominate.

Worked Example

The Dashboard That Nobody Uses

Your analytics dashboard has low engagement — only 23% of users visit it weekly. Three proposals land on your desk:

A Add more chart types and visualization options
B Make the dashboard the default landing page
C Interview 10 power users about their first action each morning, then redesign around that workflow

The product sense read: Option C starts from the User Lens. A assumes the problem is capability (Technical Lens bias). B forces engagement through exposure — a growth tactic, not a product insight. C acknowledges you don't yet know what the problem is and goes to find out.

But the strongest answer layers in the Business Lens too: in B2B, the "user" might be a marketing manager, but the "buyer" is the VP. What does the VP need to see in their first 10 seconds? That's the question that protects the renewal.

Quiz · Test Your Thinking
1 A customer requests an API endpoint for bulk data export. The technical cost is low. Your product sense alarm should ring because:
A API features are always low priority for product teams
B The request might signal they're preparing to migrate to a competitor
C APIs don't drive user engagement metrics
D Bulk operations are technically risky at scale
Correct: B. This is the Business Lens working. A low-cost request can still be a high-signal event. "I want to export all my data" is one of the strongest churn indicators in B2B SaaS. The strongest PMs don't just evaluate the request — they decode what the request means about the customer's trajectory.
2 You're prioritizing your Q4 roadmap. Which is an example of applying the Market Lens?
A Choosing the feature with the highest RICE score
B Building what engineering is most excited about
C Accelerating your attribution feature because a competitor just raised $50M in the same space
D Defaulting to what the sales team hears most often
Correct: C. The Market Lens reads competitive dynamics and timing. A competitor raising $50M signals they're about to flood the same space with engineering resources — that changes your priority calculus. D is closer to User/Business Lens (demand filtered through sales). A is a prioritization framework, not a lens. B is Technical Lens bias.
3 "Product-led growth" in B2B SaaS means:
A The product team leads the company's overall strategy
B Growth comes from the product experience itself, not primarily from sales-led motions
C Building products using agile methodology
D Letting users vote on the product roadmap
Correct: B. PLG is a go-to-market strategy where the product is the primary vehicle for acquisition, activation, and expansion. Think Slack, Figma, Notion — users adopt the tool, experience its value, then pull in teammates. It contrasts with sales-led growth (SLG) where human sellers drive deal flow. Most B2B companies use a hybrid.
Scenario Drill

Your biggest enterprise customer (15% of ARR) is threatening to churn unless you build a custom integration with their internal CRM system. Engineering estimates 6 weeks of work that would only serve this one customer.

Apply all four lenses before deciding. Use the prompts below:

User Lens What is this customer actually trying to achieve with this integration?
Business Lens What's the true cost/benefit beyond the 6 weeks of engineering?
Technical Lens Is there a platform approach that turns this one-off into a generalizable capability?
Market Lens Are other customers or prospects likely to need similar integrations?
No wrong answers — this is about practicing the lens discipline.

A strong answer touches all four:

User: Don't take the integration request at face value. What workflow are they trying to complete? Maybe they need data to flow between systems — an integration is one solution, but a CSV sync, a webhook, or a Zapier connector might solve 80% of the need at 10% of the cost.

Business: 15% of ARR is significant — but is the customer growing or flat? If they're growing, the retention value compounds. If flat, you're building a $300K feature to protect $300K of revenue. Also: what's the opportunity cost? What doesn't ship in those 6 weeks?

Technical: This is where a technical PM shines. Can you build a generic integration framework or connector API that serves this customer today and becomes a platform capability tomorrow? That turns a one-off cost into an asset.

Market: If 3 other enterprise prospects mentioned CRM integrations in sales calls last quarter, this stops being a one-customer request and becomes a market signal.

The decision likely isn't a binary yes/no — it's about finding the version of "yes" that creates leverage beyond this single customer.

Meeting Move of the Day

Next time someone proposes a feature in a meeting, ask:

"What's the user behavior we expect to change if we ship this?"

This forces the conversation from solution-space back into problem-space. It's the single most powerful question a PM can ask because it demands that the proposer articulate the theory of change — not just the deliverable, but the causal chain from feature to outcome.

Practice it once this week. Notice how it reshapes the conversation.

Glossary
Product Sense
The ability to make good product decisions with incomplete information, by synthesizing user, business, technical, and market perspectives simultaneously.
Problem Space vs Solution Space
Problem space is the set of user needs and desired outcomes. Solution space is the set of possible implementations. Strong PMs spend more time in problem space before jumping to solutions.
ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue)
The annualized value of recurring subscription revenue. The heartbeat metric of B2B SaaS. MRR × 12 = ARR, but only for recurring contracts — services and one-time fees excluded.
PLG (Product-Led Growth)
A go-to-market strategy where the product itself drives user acquisition, conversion, and expansion — contrasted with sales-led growth (SLG). Slack, Figma, and Notion are canonical examples.
Churn Signal
Any behavioral indicator that a customer may not renew. Common signals: data export requests, declining login frequency, support ticket spikes, executive sponsor departure.
