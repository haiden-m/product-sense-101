Customer Segmentation & ICPs

You can't write a good JTBD statement if you haven't decided whose job you're describing. Segmentation is where product strategy gets specific.

The Big Idea

Customer segmentation is the discipline of dividing your addressable market into groups that share characteristics that matter for your product decisions. The Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) is the sharpest expression of that discipline: it's a precise description of the customer who gets the most value from your product and gives the most value back.

Most PMs understand segmentation in theory. The failure mode is making it too broad to be useful. "Mid-market B2B companies" is a demographic description, not a segment. It doesn't tell you what to build, how to price, or what message to put on the landing page.

A strong ICP answers a specific question: If we could only sell to one type of customer for the next 12 months, who would it be — and why?

Why this matters right now. Yesterday you learned JTBD — that the same feature request means different things to different personas. Today's lesson gives you the tool to decide which personas to prioritize. Segmentation without JTBD is demographics. JTBD without segmentation is a list of jobs with no way to rank them.
Beyond Demographics

The classic segmentation mistake in B2B is stopping at firmographics — industry, company size, revenue. Those are easy to filter in a CRM, but they don't predict product behavior. Two 500-person SaaS companies can have completely different needs.

Stronger segmentation uses behavioral and needs-based axes alongside firmographics:

Behavioral
How they use your product. Power users vs light users. Self-serve vs high-touch. API-first vs UI-first. Daily active vs weekly check-in.
Needs-based
What job they're hiring you for. Execution speed vs analytical depth. Single-channel vs omnichannel. Compliance-driven vs growth-driven.
Firmographic
Company size, industry, geography, tech stack. Useful for sales targeting, weak for product decisions.
Value-based
Revenue potential, expansion likelihood, cost-to-serve. Critical for unit economics, often ignored in product planning.

The strongest product strategies segment on the first two axes and use the last two for go-to-market. What your customer does and what they need drives what you build. What they look like and what they're worth drives how you sell.

Anatomy of an ICP

A useful ICP isn't a persona poster with stock photos. It's a set of falsifiable attributes that your sales, product, and marketing teams can all use to make decisions:

Example: Marketing Automation Platform ICP

Company
B2C or D2C brand, 50–500 employees, $10M–$200M revenue
Team
3–10 person marketing team with at least one dedicated ops person
Behavior
Runs 20+ campaigns/month across email, push, and SMS. Uses data warehouse or CDP.
Need
Outgrown their starter tool. Needs multi-channel orchestration, not just email blasts.
Trigger
Hitting a ceiling with current tool (Mailchimp, Klaviyo). VP hired to "professionalize" marketing.
Anti-signal
No dedicated ops resource. Fewer than 5 campaigns/month. No existing data infrastructure.

Notice the anti-signals. An ICP that only describes who you want attracts false positives. The anti-signals are equally important — they're the customers who look right on paper but will churn in 6 months because the product is too complex for their maturity level.

The ICP Pressure Test

Most ICPs are too soft. They describe the customer in ways that feel true but don't constrain decisions. Pressure-test yours with these five questions:

  • Does it exclude someone? An ICP that describes 80% of your market isn't a profile — it's a TAM definition. If it doesn't make you uncomfortable leaving money on the table, it's not sharp enough.
  • Can sales qualify against it in one call? If your ICP requires three discovery calls to evaluate, its attributes are too abstract. Each dimension should be verifiable.
  • Does it predict retention? Map your ICP against actual churn data. The customers who match should retain better than those who don't. If there's no correlation, the ICP is describing the wrong things.
  • Does it change what you build? If your roadmap would be identical regardless of which ICP you chose, the segmentation isn't influencing product decisions.
  • Would your top 10 customers recognize themselves? Show your ICP to your best customers. If they say "that's not quite us," you've defined a theoretical customer, not a real one.
Worked Example

The Segment That Shouldn't Have Been One Segment

Your B2B platform groups all "enterprise" customers together — anyone paying over $100K/year. Product planning treats them as one segment. But looking deeper:

Segment as-is
"Enterprise customers" — 45 accounts, $100K+ ARR, 500+ employees. Broad feature requests: more integrations, better analytics, SSO.
After behavioral split
Enterprise-Ops (28 accounts): API-heavy, data warehouse connected, want automation and control. Enterprise-Lite (17 accounts): UI-only, limited tech team, want simplicity and templates.

The "more integrations" request was coming from Enterprise-Ops. The "better analytics" request was coming from Enterprise-Lite — they wanted simpler analytics, not more powerful ones. Building one "enterprise analytics" feature would have half-satisfied both and fully satisfied neither.

The behavioral split revealed two distinct ICPs with conflicting jobs-to-be-done. Product strategy for each is fundamentally different: one needs a platform, the other needs a guided product.

Scenario Drill

You're the PM at a B2B marketing platform. Your sales team closes anyone willing to pay — from 5-person startups to 10,000-person enterprises. The result: your support queue is overwhelmed, feature requests pull in every direction, and churn is 18% annually. The CEO asks you to define the ICP to focus the company.

Draft an ICP with at least 5 dimensions. Include anti-signals. Then explain: which current customers does this ICP exclude, and how do you handle the internal pushback from sales when you tell them to stop closing certain deals?

The hard part isn't writing the ICP — it's defending it to the sales team that just brought in a $200K deal from a customer who doesn't match.

Step 1: Look at the data, not the deals. Pull retention by cohort and segment. You'll almost certainly find that one behavioral cluster retains at 90%+ while another churns at 30%+. The high-retention cluster is your ICP draft.

Step 2: Define the ICP from the cluster. Suppose the data shows that customers who run 15+ campaigns/month, have an ops person, and connect a data source retain at 92%. Those behavioral attributes become your ICP — with their common firmographics (B2C, 100-1000 employees, $20M+ revenue) as the targeting layer on top.

Step 3: Define the anti-signals explicitly. No dedicated marketing ops person. Fewer than 5 campaigns/month. No existing data infrastructure. Buying for a single-channel use case only. These predict the 30% churn cluster.

Step 4: Handle the sales pushback. Don't tell sales to "stop closing deals." Instead, present the data: "Customers who match our ICP have a 92% retention rate and expand 30% year-over-year. Customers who don't match churn at 30% and generate 4x the support tickets. Every bad-fit deal costs us $X in support and delays features for the customers who actually stay." Make it a math argument, not a philosophy argument.

Step 5: Create a middle path. For borderline customers, define a "qualified with conditions" tier — they can sign, but with a different onboarding track, different SLA expectations, and a different CSM approach. You're not rejecting revenue; you're risk-adjusting how you serve it.

Meeting Move of the Day

When someone advocates for a feature by saying "our customers want this," ask:

"Which customers — and would we sign more of them today, knowing what we know?"

This does two things. First, it forces specificity — "our customers" becomes "three enterprise accounts in retail." Second, it introduces the ICP lens: are those the customers we're building for, or the ones we signed before we had focus? The answer changes the priority.

Glossary
Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)
A precise description of the customer who gets the most value from your product and returns the most value to your business. Defined by behavioral, needs-based, firmographic, and value attributes.
Anti-signal
A customer attribute that predicts poor fit — high churn risk, excessive support cost, or product-diluting feature requests. As important as positive ICP attributes.
NRR (Net Revenue Retention)
Revenue from existing customers after accounting for expansion, contraction, and churn. NRR above 100% means your customer base grows even without new sales. The single most important SaaS health metric after product-market fit.
Behavioral Segmentation
Grouping customers by what they do with your product (usage patterns, feature adoption, engagement frequency) rather than what they look like (size, industry). Predicts product needs better than firmographics.
Cost-to-Serve
The total cost of supporting a customer — onboarding, support tickets, CSM time, custom work. High cost-to-serve customers can be profitable or unprofitable depending on their contract value and expansion potential.
