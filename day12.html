Opportunity Sizing & Cost of Delay

Every backlog item has a size. Only some of them have a clock attached. Yesterday's frameworks rank value against effort — none of them tell you what waiting costs.

Yesterday you learned to match RICE, ICE, or MoSCoW to the kind of decision in front of you — ranking a mature backlog, triaging raw ideas, or negotiating a forced cut. All three answer the same question: how big is this, relative to the others? None of them ask the question underneath that one: what do we lose if we don't ship it this month instead of next? That's today's gap — sizing the opportunity honestly, and pricing the delay.
01

The Big Idea

Opportunity Sizing and Cost of Delay are two separate disciplines that most roadmap conversations collapse into one fuzzy word: "impact." Separating them is what lets you catch the single most expensive prioritization mistake in B2B SaaS — ranking by how big something is while ignoring how fast its value is leaking away.

Opportunity Sizing is not the TAM/SAM/SOM exercise from Day 8. Market sizing answers "how big is the market this product category could ever serve." Opportunity Sizing answers a narrower, more useful question: "if we solve this specific problem for this specific segment, what is it realistically worth" — in expansion revenue, retained accounts, or reduced cost. It's sizing one initiative, not one market, and it's the number RICE's "Impact" and "Reach" inputs should actually be built from rather than guessed.

Cost of Delay (CoD) adds the dimension every static scoring framework leaves out: time. It is the economic value an opportunity loses, per week or per quarter, for every period you don't ship it. Two opportunities can have the same total size and completely different urgency — one bleeds value the moment it's delayed, the other barely changes whether you ship it this month or next.

Why this isn't just RICE with an extra column

RICE and ICE are both snapshot tools — they score an opportunity as if it were frozen in time. Cost of Delay is a rate. Once you have a rate, you can combine it with how long the work takes to produce CD3 (Cost of Delay ÷ Duration), sometimes called WSJF — Weighted Shortest Job First, the term SAFe and Donald Reinertsen's writing use for it. CD3 answers the question RICE structurally can't: of two opportunities, which one returns more economic value per week of work the team actually spends — which is often the smaller, faster, more urgent one, not the bigger one.

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

A B2B SaaS product is choosing between two items for next sprint. The VP wants "the bigger opportunity" built first.

"Cross-channel personalization engine" is the obvious pick — it's worth $600K a year once fully adopted. The data-residency fix for EU accounts is worth a fraction of that. Build the big one first.

Sizing each honestly, including how its value decays over time:

EU data-residency fixstep-function — value is flat, then falls off a cliff
Cross-channel personalization enginelinear — value accrues steadily, no hard deadline

Pricing each with CD3 before deciding what "bigger" means:

OpportunityCost of DelayDurationCD3 (CoD ÷ Duration)
EU data-residency fix$40K/wk after wk 83 weeks13.3
Personalization engine$11K/wk, steady10 weeks1.1

The reframe: the personalization engine is the bigger total opportunity — nobody disputes that. But its cost of delay is nearly flat this quarter: shipping it in week 11 instead of week 1 costs roughly ten weeks of a slow linear bleed. The residency fix is small in total size but has a step-function profile: miss the regulatory deadline and several EU accounts become contractually required to leave, and the exposure jumps from near-zero to $40K a week overnight. It's also the shorter build. CD3 makes the sequencing obvious where "which one's bigger" didn't: ship the fix first, not because it's more valuable overall, but because it returns far more economic value per week of work the team has left before the deadline turns a flat line into a cliff.

Scenario Drill

The situation Your B2B SaaS product, planning next quarter. Two items are competing for the same two engineers: Model accuracy improvements — steady uplift worth roughly $25K/week once live, no hard deadline — and a payment-provider API migration forced because the processor your billing flow depends on is sunsetting its API in 12 weeks. After that date, renewals and upgrades stop processing, full stop. Your VP asks: "Which has the bigger opportunity — let's resource that one first." What do you actually tell them?

Step 1 — Notice the question is already wrong

  • "Bigger opportunity" is a sizing question. It assumes both items are being compared on the same axis — total value — when one of them isn't really a prioritization decision at all. If billing stops processing renewals for every customer in 12 weeks, that's not an opportunity to size against another opportunity; it's a deadline the business cannot miss.

Step 2 — Size both honestly, including the shape of the delay cost

  • Model accuracy improvements: real, recurring value ($25K/week once live), but its Cost of Delay curve is close to flat — shipping it in week 14 instead of week 2 costs roughly twelve weeks of foregone uplift, which is real money but not catastrophic, and nothing breaks if it waits.
  • Payment-provider migration: Cost of Delay is close to $0/week for the first 11 weeks, then becomes effectively unbounded in week 13 — total loss of a core revenue path for every customer, not a percentage hit. That's a step-function profile with the step occurring at the edge of your delivery window, which is the specific case CD3 was built to catch: a shorter job whose cost of delay is about to go vertical outranks a bigger job whose cost of delay is still flat.

Step 3 — Reframe the decision for the VP

  • "Model improvements are the bigger number on a spreadsheet, but they don't have a clock on them — we lose a little value every week we delay, and we can recover that later. The payment-provider migration has a hard deadline in 12 weeks; past that, renewals stop processing for every customer, and there's no recovering that after the fact. This isn't really 'which is the bigger opportunity' — it's 'which one turns into an outage if we guess wrong.' We migrate first, and we scope model work for right after."

The trap this drill is testing

  • Total opportunity size feels like the more sophisticated answer because it's a bigger, more exciting number. The senior move is recognizing that a Cost of Delay curve with a cliff in it isn't competing with a flat one on the same terms — it has effectively already won the sequencing question, and the real job is explaining why to a VP who's reaching for the top-line number.
MEETING MOVE

Use this in your next roadmap or planning meeting

Next time two items are compared only by size, redirect with:

"Before we compare how big these are — what's the cost of delay on each one? If we ship this in Q2 instead of Q1, what specifically do we lose, and does that loss grow steadily, suddenly, or not at all?"
05

Glossary

Opportunity Sizing
Estimating the realistic value of solving one specific problem for one specific segment — distinct from market sizing (TAM/SAM/SOM), which estimates how big an entire market could ever be.
Cost of Delay (CoD)
The quantified economic impact — in revenue, risk, or cost — of not shipping something, expressed as a rate per unit time rather than a one-time total.
CD3 / WSJF
Cost of Delay divided by Duration. A sequencing score, popularized as Weighted Shortest Job First in SAFe and Donald Reinertsen's work, that favors fast-to-ship, urgent value over slower, larger value.
Cost of Delay Profile
The shape of how an opportunity's delay cost accrues over time: linear (steady), step-function or time-critical (flat, then a cliff at a deadline), or intangible/long-tail (hard to quantify but still real, like brand or trust erosion).
Opportunity Decay
The erosion of an initiative's value the longer it sits unbuilt — a competitor closes the window, a regulatory grace period ends, or user intent moves on. The mechanism that makes Cost of Delay nonzero in the first place.
