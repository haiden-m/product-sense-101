Problem Space vs Solution Space

The single sentence that separates a PM who explores from one who just orders features off a menu.

So far: product sense, first principles, jobs-to-be-done, and naming your ICP precisely → Today, the discipline that ties them together: staying in the problem space long enough to actually need the segmentation and the JTBD work you just did.
I. The Big Idea 5 min read

Two spaces, and almost every roadmap fight is about which one you're standing in

This week you've built three tools for understanding a customer: product sense (Day 1), first principles decomposition (Day 2), and jobs-to-be-done (Day 3). Day 4 sharpened who you're studying — your ICP. Today's lesson is about discipline, not a new analytical tool: knowing which of two spaces you're currently reasoning in, and refusing to let them blur together.

The problem space is everything true about the customer's world before you've committed to a fix: their goals, their constraints, what's actually broken, who's affected, how often, and how badly. The solution space is the set of specific things you could build to address it — features, workflows, UI, architecture. Product sense, first principles, and JTBD are all tools for exploring the problem space well. The mistake that undoes all three is jumping to the solution space before the problem space is actually understood — usually because a solution arrived first, dressed up as a request.

Problem space
"Enterprise marketers at regulated companies can't launch a compliant campaign without a 3-day legal review cycle, and that review is manual and inconsistent across reviewers."
Narrowing
The reviewable unit is campaign copy + audience + offer terms; the bottleneck is reviewer availability and inconsistent judgment, not the review step itself.
Solution space
Rule-based pre-checks that auto-clear 70% of campaigns, an audit trail for the rest, a reviewer queue with SLA tracking. (A dedicated "approval workflow" is one shape this could take — not the only one.)

Notice the width of each bar. The problem space is intentionally wide — it holds every fact about the situation, including ones that turn out irrelevant. The solution space narrows only after you've explored enough of the problem space to know what actually matters. Jump straight to the bottom row and you'll build a plausible-sounding feature that may not touch the real bottleneck at all.

A request is a hypothesis about the solution space wearing the clothes of a fact about the problem space. Your job is to take the clothes off before you scope anything.

This is where Tuesday's and Wednesday's tools do their real work. First principles decomposition is how you strip a request back to problem-space facts. JTBD is how you describe the problem space in terms of progress the customer is trying to make, not features they're missing. Staying disciplined about which space you're in isn't a fourth framework — it's the rule that makes the other three actually get used, instead of skipped because "we already know what to build."

II. Worked Example 3 min
B2B SaaS · CRM Marketing Platform

"Add a scheduling calendar view to the campaign dashboard"

A mid-market retail customer's marketing ops lead submits this through your feedback portal, upvoted by six other accounts. It reads like a clean, specific, buildable request — exactly the kind that's tempting to drop straight into a sprint.

Here's how a senior PM keeps it in the problem space one extra beat before deciding anything:

1
Name what space the request is written in. "Calendar view" is a UI pattern — pure solution space. The problem space question underneath it hasn't been asked yet: what decision or action does the marketer need this calendar to support?
2
Go back to the problem space with a targeted question. Talking to three of the six upvoters reveals the actual friction: marketers run 15-20 campaigns a month across email, SMS, and push, and currently can't tell at a glance whether two campaigns will hit the same VIP segment on the same day — leading to over-messaging complaints.
3
Confirm the narrowed problem before touching solutions. The real problem-space fact: overlap detection across concurrent sends to shared audiences, not visualization of dates. A calendar view could surface this — but so could a proactive overlap warning at scheduling time, which needs no new screen at all.
4
Only now, enter the solution space — deliberately. The team ships an audience-overlap warning inline in the existing scheduler: two engineering weeks instead of a new calendar surface, and it fixes the complaint the six upvoters actually had, which a calendar view alone would only have partially addressed.

IV. Scenario Drill 8 min
The situation Your Head of Sales forwards you an email from a prospect evaluating your platform against two competitors: "We need a native Salesforce integration with two-way field sync before we can sign. Every vendor we're looking at has one. Can we commit to Q3?" Sales wants this greenlit in this week's roadmap review — it's framed as a $400K deal blocker.
Before you write "native Salesforce integration" into the roadmap, work it yourself: what problem-space question haven't you asked yet, and why does the answer matter more than matching what "every vendor" has?
Senior PM analysis
Spot the solution-space language doing the talking

"Native integration with two-way field sync" is a specific architecture — a solution. It arrived without a stated problem attached, other than "everyone else has it," which is market signal about frequency, not a fact about what this prospect's team actually needs synced, how often, or why.

Go back into the problem space

A 20-minute call with the prospect's ops lead reveals the real friction: their sales reps manually re-enter campaign engagement data into Salesforce every Monday to prioritize outbound calls, and it's error-prone and a day behind. The underlying job is "give reps same-day visibility into marketing engagement inside the tool they already live in" — which is narrower than full bidirectional sync of every field.

Reintroduce "every vendor has one" — correctly scoped

The competitive signal tells you integration depth matters to this ICP segment broadly — worth investing in. It doesn't tell you two-way sync is the right first release. A one-way, near-real-time push of engagement events into Salesforce (a much smaller build) may satisfy the actual job for this quarter, with full two-way sync as a phase 2 the team can commit to honestly instead of overpromising Q3.

Take the narrowed answer back to Sales

The move isn't to say no to the deal blocker — it's to come back with "here's what we can ship by Q3 that solves the actual workflow gap your ops lead described, and here's the fuller sync as a committed phase 2." That's a stronger, more credible answer than agreeing to "two-way sync" and discovering the real scope in week six of the build.

V. Meeting Move 2 min

Use this the moment a request arrives already shaped as a feature

"If we set aside [the specific feature] for a second — what's the situation this needs to fix, and how would we know it was fixed even if the solution looked completely different?"

This question does the space-separation work in one move: it forces the room to state the problem-space fact independently of the proposed fix, and the "how would we know" clause gives you a success criterion you can test against multiple solution shapes — not just the one that was pitched to you.

VI. Glossary 2 min
01 Problem space
The set of facts about a customer's situation, goals, and constraints — independent of any proposed fix. Where discovery, JTBD interviews, and first-principles decomposition live.
02 Solution space
The set of specific things you could build to address a problem — features, UI, architecture. Should be entered deliberately, after the problem space is understood, not by default.
03 Solutioning (premature)
Committing to a specific build before the underlying problem has been validated or scoped. The most common way roadmaps fill up with features that solve the wrong-sized version of a real problem.
04 Requirement vs. request
A request names a solution ("add a calendar view"); a requirement names a problem-space fact the solution must satisfy ("marketers need to detect audience overlap before sending"). Translating the former into the latter is the core discipline of this lesson.
05 Success criterion (space-independent)
A way of knowing a problem is solved that doesn't reference the specific solution built — e.g., "over-messaging complaints drop by half," not "the calendar view shipped." Lets you validate outcomes even if you change the solution mid-flight.
