Technical Debt as Product Strategy

Yesterday sorted decisions by how expensive they are to walk back. Today applies that same question to the code and architecture decisions piling up under your roadmap: debt isn't a confession engineering makes to you — it's a loan you and engineering take out together, and your job is knowing the interest rate.

Yesterday you learned to ask, before a decision, how expensive it is to reverse. Technical debt is what happens when nobody asked that question at the moment the shortcut was taken — and some of that debt turns out to have been a one-way door all along, just one that took eighteen months to notice. A shortcut scoped behind a flag and ripped out next sprint is debt you can walk back cheaply. A shortcut baked into a data schema, a public API, or three other services' assumptions is debt that locked the door behind you before anyone called it a decision. Today gives you the vocabulary to tell, at the moment debt is proposed, which kind you're about to take on.
01

The Big Idea

Ward Cunningham coined "technical debt" in 1992, and the metaphor was never meant as an insult. A loan lets you do something today you couldn't otherwise afford; the problem was never borrowing, it was borrowing without tracking the balance or the rate.

Debt, in this framing, has a principal — what it costs to actually fix the shortcut later — and interest — the recurring tax you pay every sprint it stays unfixed: slower feature velocity in that area, a higher bug rate, longer onboarding for new engineers who have to learn the workaround before they can learn the system. A wise loan is one where the interest rate is low and known, and you deliberately chose it to buy something worth more than the interest: an enterprise renewal, a market window, a chance to learn whether customers even want the feature before investing in the "real" version.

Martin Fowler later split the metaphor into a quadrant that most product orgs still collapse into one bucket. The question isn't "is this debt bad" — it's two separate questions: was it taken on deliberately or did it happen inadvertently (nobody decided to borrow, it just accumulated), and was the decision prudent or reckless given what was known at the time.

Deliberate + prudent

The team knows the shortcut, names it in the ticket, and ships it to hit a real deadline with a plan to repay. This is strategy, not failure — the PM's job is making sure the repayment actually gets scheduled instead of living forever as "someday."

Inadvertent + prudent

Debt that only becomes visible in hindsight — the design was sound for what the team knew at the time, but the product grew in a direction that exposed it. No one to blame; the move is to re-evaluate now that you know more.

Deliberate + reckless

"There's no time to design it properly, just ship something" with no plan to revisit. The team knew it was a shortcut and took it anyway without naming the cost — this is the quadrant a PM should push back on in the room, not after the incident.

Inadvertent + reckless

Nobody decided anything; the shortcuts just compounded because no one was watching the balance. This is where most catastrophic debt actually lives — invisible until a production incident forces a reckoning on a bill nobody knew was accruing.

Your job isn't zero debt

A roadmap with zero technical debt is usually a roadmap that moved too slowly to learn anything. The discipline is treating debt like a CFO treats a balance sheet: know what you've borrowed, know the rate, and decide deliberately when the interest payments exceed what the loan bought you. "Pay down all debt" and "ignore all debt" are both abdications of that job — the actual work is triage.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

A platform team's messaging module takes on two debts in the same quarter — and only tracks one of them.

"We hardcoded the VIP-segment exception straight into the send pipeline to hit the enterprise renewal. Everyone knows it's there — it's in the ticket. Separately, nobody had time to design a shared event contract, so each of the three services parses the webhook payload its own way. That one's not really a decision, it just happened."
DebtQuadrantInterest, paid wherePay down now?
VIP-segment logic hardcoded into the send pipeline to hit a renewal deadlineDeliberate + prudentOne documented workaround; mild slowdown adding the next segment exceptionNo — scheduled for next quarter, tracked
Three services independently parsing the same webhook payload, no shared contractInadvertent + recklessEvery schema change now means three inconsistent fixes instead of one — and nobody flagged it as debt at allYes — this is the one compounding unnoticed

The reframe: the VIP exception sounds like the riskier shortcut because it was fast and visible, but it's a loan the team took out with open eyes, documented, and scheduled for repayment — a solved problem wearing a scary name. The webhook parsing never got a ticket, a name, or a line item anywhere, because nobody experienced it as a decision — it was three separate "there's no time, I'll just parse it myself" moments that never got added up. By the time a schema change broke two of the three implementations differently, the interest due dwarfed what a thirty-minute shared-contract conversation would have cost. Debt you name is debt you can manage. Debt that happens to you is the kind that sends a production incident instead of a ticket.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation Engineering says the legacy campaign-orchestration service needs a three-sprint rewrite before the new real-time trigger feature can ship safely — the current service was built years ago for scheduled, batch-style sends, and bolting real-time triggers onto it directly means every future incident touches code nobody fully understands anymore. Sales is pushing to ship real-time triggers in three weeks to close a competitive deal, and says "just bolt it on for this one customer, we'll clean it up later." Is engineering's debt-payoff request itself prudent or reckless, and what do you tell Sales?

Step 1 — Classify the debt that's already there

  • The orchestration service wasn't built recklessly — batch sends were the whole product at the time, and a scheduled-send architecture was the prudent choice for that scope. This debt is inadvertent + prudent: nobody did anything wrong, the product just grew past what the original design anticipated. That matters, because it means the reframe isn't "engineering should have known better," it's "now we know more, and the decision is whether to act on it."

Step 2 — Name what "bolt it on" actually proposes

  • Sales's plan isn't a new loan against a known interest rate — it's building a second shortcut directly on top of unaddressed debt, for one customer, without naming the cost. That is the deliberate + reckless quadrant: everyone will know a shortcut was taken, and nobody will have sized what it costs going forward. Once a second customer wants real-time triggers, "for this one customer" becomes the architecture, and unwinding it costs more than the original three-sprint estimate.

Step 3 — Separate the one-way-door part from the two-way-door part

  • Whether to eventually rewrite the orchestration service is a two-way door — it's internal, invisible to customers, reversible at the cost of engineering time. Whether to ship a customer-facing real-time trigger feature built on an unstable interim layer is closer to a one-way door, because customers will start depending on its exact behavior the moment it ships, the same way partners depended on the webhook schema in Day 14's example.

Step 4 — Bring Sales a real option, not just "no"

  • "The three-sprint rewrite is the clean path, but I don't want to make Sales wait on an architecture decision when there's a scoped alternative: a one-sprint wrapper that handles real-time triggers for this single customer's specific trigger conditions, clearly logged as a temporary bridge, with the full rewrite already on the roadmap for next quarter. That gets the deal closed without quietly making the workaround the permanent design — the debt stays named and scheduled instead of becoming the dangerous, nobody's-tracking-it kind."

The trap this drill is testing

  • "We'll clean it up later" is the sentence every piece of reckless, inadvertent debt was originally described as. The senior move isn't refusing the shortcut or rubber-stamping it — it's making sure that if a shortcut gets taken, it gets taken the way the VIP-segment exception did: named, ticketed, and given an actual repayment date, not left to compound until it becomes somebody else's incident.
MEETING MOVE

Use this when a shortcut is being proposed

Next time someone says "we'll just do it quick and clean it up later," redirect with:

"What's the interest rate on this — what does it cost us every sprint it stays unpaid — and who's putting a repayment date on the calendar right now, before we walk out of this room?"
05

Glossary

Technical Debt
The implied future cost of choosing a faster, easier implementation now over a more thorough one that would take longer. Not inherently bad — a tool, like financial debt, that's prudent or reckless depending on whether it's tracked and repaid.
Debt Quadrant
Martin Fowler's framework classifying debt along two axes: deliberate vs. inadvertent (was it a conscious choice) and prudent vs. reckless (was it a sound choice given what was known). The dangerous quadrant is inadvertent + reckless — debt nobody decided to take on and nobody is tracking.
Interest (Technical)
The recurring cost a piece of debt imposes every period it remains unpaid — slower velocity in that area, elevated bug rates, steeper onboarding for new engineers. The number that should drive whether and when to repay, not how old or embarrassing the debt feels.
Principal vs. Interest
Principal is the one-time cost to actually fix a piece of debt; interest is the ongoing tax of leaving it unfixed. The repayment decision is a comparison of cumulative interest against principal, the same math as any other loan.
Refactor vs. Rewrite
Two different debt-repayment strategies: a refactor incrementally improves structure without changing external behavior; a rewrite replaces the implementation wholesale. Rewrites carry higher risk and a longer payoff horizon, and are usually a last resort rather than a first instinct.
