The Pyramid Principle

Yesterday's reasoned no had a structure you didn't name: the decision first, the reasons after. That's not a coincidence — it's the same architecture Barbara Minto built for McKinsey consultants, and it's the one structural habit that upgrades every PRD, Slack update, and stakeholder email you write from here on. Today you name it and start building it on purpose.

Yesterday you learned that a reasoned no works because it leads with the decision and backs it with specific, structured reasons — trade-off, standard, door. Strip away the context of "saying no" and what's left is a pyramid: one conclusion at the top, a small set of reasons underneath it. Today's lesson is the general-purpose version of that same shape, so you can build it deliberately into anything you write, not just declines.
01

The Big Idea

Most people think by gathering evidence and building up to a conclusion — and then they write the same way, in the order they figured it out. That's backwards for anyone reading you on a phone between meetings. The Pyramid Principle doesn't change how you think; it changes the order you present it in. Think bottom-up. Write top-down.

A pyramid has three tiers, and skipping the order breaks the whole thing:

Governing Thought The single sentence that answers "so what" — stated first, before any reason appears
Key Line 2–4 grouped arguments that support the governing thought, each a complete reason, not a topic label
Supporting Data The evidence, examples, and detail underneath each key-line argument — read only by whoever wants to verify it
  • Governing Thought first. State the answer or recommendation in the first sentence, before the reader has any reasons to evaluate it against. Burying it rewards skimming with the wrong takeaway.
  • MECE groupings. The key-line arguments should be Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive — no two reasons overlapping, and nothing relevant left standing outside the list. A reader who senses a gap starts doubting the whole recommendation.
  • SCQA opens the runway. Situation (shared context), Complication (what changed), Question (the one your reader is now implicitly asking), Answer (your governing thought). Four sentences, and the reader arrives at your conclusion instead of being told to accept it.
  • Detail is opt-in, not mandatory reading. Supporting data belongs under its key-line argument, not ahead of it — so a reader who trusts your groupings can stop after the key line, and one who doesn't can drill down exactly where their doubt is.
— The Narrative Draft "So I wanted to walk you through what we found this week..."

Written in discovery order — the order the PM learned things, not the order the reader needs them. The recommendation, if there is one, shows up in the last paragraph, after the reader has already formed their own.

What happens: busy readers skim to the end, or stop partway and reply with a question your third paragraph already answered.

+ The Pyramid Draft "We should delay the EU launch three weeks. Three reasons:"

Opens with the governing thought, then three MECE reasons, each with its own evidence underneath for anyone who wants to check it.

What happens: the reader has the decision in one sentence and chooses, themselves, how deep to read.

Yesterday's reasoned no was a pyramid you'd already built

Day 20's four-part no had a governing thought (the decision) and a key line underneath it (the trade-off, the standard, the door) — you just hadn't named the shape. The Pyramid Principle is that same architecture applied everywhere else: Slack updates, PRDs, exec summaries, roadmap narratives. Once you can name the governing thought and check your reasons for MECE, you stop discovering your point mid-paragraph and start deciding it before you type the first word.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

Your team's Salesforce sync integration — promised for a flagship account's go-live — is going to slip two weeks. You need to tell the VP of Sales today. Two ways to write that Slack message:

What happenedBottom-up messagePyramid-structured message
Time for the VP to find the actual ask~45 sec, scrolling through 6 paragraphsImmediate — first sentence
Clarifying replies needed4 ("wait, so are we delayed or not?")0
VP's next actionForwarded the thread to their boss: "what's going on here?"Replied in 2 minutes: "approved, use the extra engineer"
How the PM came acrossBuried in details, unclear on the askIn command of the situation
Time the PM spent writing it25 min, three rewrites, still a little murky12 min, once the governing thought was decided first

The reframe: the pyramid version wasn't just easier to read — it was faster to write. Deciding the governing thought first ("we need two weeks and one borrowed engineer") forces the hard thinking before you type a word. Writing bottom-up feels natural because you're discovering your own point as you go, which is exactly why those drafts run long, circle back, and still leave the reader guessing at the end.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation You're about to message your VP of Product with three findings from this week, in the order you found them:
  • Mid-market churn is up 2 points quarter-over-quarter, concentrated in accounts on the legacy pricing tier.
  • Support ticket volume for those same accounts is up 40%, mostly complaints about a UI change shipped last quarter.
  • Three of the top five churned accounts never completed onboarding into the new reporting dashboard.
Before you send anything: what's the governing thought, what are the MECE key-line arguments underneath it, and what does the actual message say?

The governing thought to avoid

  • "Mid-market churn is up 2 points" is a fact, not a governing thought — it doesn't tell the VP what to believe or do. A governing thought is a claim with a direction attached.
  • "We should reprice the legacy tier" jumps to a solution before the data supports it — nothing here shows a pricing problem specifically.

The governing thought that fits the evidence

  • "Mid-market churn is concentrated in under-onboarded, legacy-tier accounts hit hardest by last quarter's UI change — this is a support and onboarding fix, not a pricing or fit problem."

MECE key line underneath it

  • Where it's concentrated: legacy-tier accounts specifically, not the broader mid-market segment — rules out a general fit problem.
  • Why those accounts: the UI change hit their existing workflows hardest, visible directly in the 40% ticket spike.
  • What's different about the ones who left: they skipped onboarding into the new reporting dashboard, so they never saw the value that would have offset the UI friction.

The actual message

"Mid-market churn this quarter is concentrated in legacy-tier accounts, and it's an onboarding and support problem, not a pricing one — here's why. First, it's isolated to the legacy tier, not the broader segment. Second, those accounts' ticket volume is up 40%, almost entirely tied to last quarter's UI change. Third, 3 of the top 5 churned accounts never finished onboarding into the new reporting dashboard, so they never got the value that would've offset the friction. Proposing a targeted onboarding push plus updated support docs for the legacy tier before we touch pricing — can scope that by Friday."
MEETING MOVE

Use this before you send any update, PRD section, or Slack message longer than three sentences

Ask it of your own draft, or of a teammate's, before it goes out:

"What's your governing thought — the one sentence that would still be true and still be useful if I deleted everything else in this message?"
05

Glossary

Governing Thought
The single top-level conclusion or recommendation that answers "so what" for the reader, stated before any supporting reason appears — the apex of the pyramid.
Key Line
The 2–4 grouped arguments directly beneath the governing thought. Each one is a complete reason that could stand on its own, not a vague topic label like "data" or "risks."
MECE
Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive — the test for a well-formed grouping of arguments or categories: no two overlap, and nothing relevant is left uncovered.
SCQA
Situation, Complication, Question, Answer — a four-part opening that walks the reader from shared context to your governing thought, so they arrive at your conclusion instead of being asked to simply accept it.
Top-Down Communication
Presenting the answer first and the reasoning after, as distinct from bottom-up (chronological, discovery-order) writing, which makes the reader rebuild your thinking before they can find your point.
