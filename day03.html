Week 1 · The PM Mental Model

Jobs-to-be-Done

People don't buy products. They hire them to make progress. Learn the framework that turns vague feature requests into precise product strategy.

The Big Idea

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) says that people don't buy products for what they are — they "hire" them for the progress they're trying to make. The classic version: "People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole." But JTBD goes deeper — they don't even want the hole. They want to hang a shelf. And they want to hang a shelf because they want their living room to feel organized.

The job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. And every job operates on three layers:

Functional
The practical task. "Organize my campaign performance data into a report."
Emotional
How they want to feel. "Confident that I'm making the right budget allocation."
Social
How they want to be perceived. "Seen as data-driven and strategic by my VP."

Most PMs only hear the functional layer. But the emotional and social layers often drive the buying decision — and the churn decision. A report that's technically accurate but makes the marketer feel uncertain about what to do next has failed the emotional job, even if it nailed the functional one.

The JTBD Statement

JTBD gives you a precise sentence format for capturing what users actually need. It's more useful than a user story because it grounds the need in a real moment:

When I'm preparing for my weekly marketing review,
I want to quickly see which campaigns are underperforming,
So I can reallocate budget before the meeting and look prepared.

Compare that to a typical user story: "As a marketer, I want a dashboard so I can see my data." The JTBD version tells you the circumstance (weekly review prep), the motivation (spot underperformers fast), and the outcome (reallocate budget, appear prepared). It's specific enough to design from.

The Four Forces of Progress

JTBD also explains why people switch products — or don't. When someone adopts a new tool, four forces are at play:

Push
Dissatisfaction with the current solution. "This takes me 2 hours every Monday."
Pull
Attraction of the new solution. "That tool generates the report in 5 minutes."
Anxiety
Uncertainty about switching. "Will my historical data migrate correctly?"
Habit
Comfort with the status quo. "I already know how to build this in Excel."
Switching happens when Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit

This is why B2B products with high switching costs retain customers even when better alternatives exist — Anxiety and Habit are too strong. It's also why your onboarding experience matters as much as your feature set: great onboarding reduces Anxiety.

Competing against non-consumption. Sometimes your biggest competitor isn't another product — it's doing nothing. The prospect solves the problem with spreadsheets, email, and manual processes. The job exists, but they haven't hired anything to do it. That means your Push messaging needs to make the pain of the status quo visible, because they've normalized it.
Worked Example

"I Need Better Reports"

A customer submits this request. Here's what the JTBD lens reveals:

Functional
"I need to understand which campaigns drove revenue last month."
Emotional
"I want to feel confident presenting numbers to my CMO."
Social
"I want to be the person who always has the data."

The full JTBD statement:

When preparing for my monthly CMO review, I want to quickly generate a campaign attribution report with revenue impact, So I can demonstrate marketing's contribution to pipeline and feel confident in my presentation.

This tells you far more than "better reports":

  • Timing: monthly cadence, preparation context
  • Format: needs to be presentation-ready, not raw data
  • Metric: revenue attribution specifically
  • Audience: CMO — executive summary, not granular detail
  • Emotion: confidence and credibility are the real deliverable
Quiz · Test Your Thinking
1 "When I'm running late for a meeting, I want to grab something filling quickly, so I can get through the morning without being hungry." In JTBD terms, the "job" is:
ABuying breakfast
BGetting through a time-constrained morning without hunger
CGoing to a coffee shop
DEating a healthy meal
Correct: B. The job is the progress in a particular circumstance. The circumstance (running late) shapes everything — it's why a banana, a protein bar, and a drive-through milkshake all compete for the same job despite being in completely different product categories. "Breakfast" is the solution space. The job is the outcome.
2 A B2B customer says "I want a Salesforce integration." The most useful JTBD follow-up question is:
A"Which Salesforce edition are you on?"
B"What workflow does this need to support — what are you trying to accomplish that requires data flowing between the two systems?"
C"Have you seen our competitor's Salesforce integration?"
D"How many users would need access?"
Correct: B. JTBD pushes you upstream of the solution. A, C, and D accept the integration as a given and optimize around it. B goes to the job: maybe the real need is a daily summary email with CRM data, which is far simpler to build. You can't discover that if you never ask what progress they're trying to make.
3 The Four Forces model says switching happens when:
AThe new product has more features than the old one
BPush from current pain + Pull from the new solution exceeds Anxiety about switching + Habit of the current way
CThe price of the new product is lower than the current one
DA competitor raises funding and increases marketing spend
Correct: B. The Four Forces model captures why switching is an emotional and behavioral challenge, not just a feature comparison. This explains why B2B products with high switching costs (data migration, retraining, workflow disruption) retain customers even when superior alternatives exist — the Anxiety and Habit forces overpower the Pull.
Scenario Drill

You're a PM at a marketing automation platform. Your sales team keeps losing deals to a competitor with "better AI features." Before adding AI to the roadmap, you want to understand the actual jobs-to-be-done.

Write a JTBD statement for each persona. Where do their jobs conflict?

Marketing Manager (daily user)
What's their day-to-day frustration? What moment would trigger them to reach for AI?
VP of Marketing (buyer / decision maker)
What story do they need to tell? Who's their audience?
Marketing Ops Admin (implementer)
What does adopting new AI capabilities mean for their workload?
Think functional, emotional, and social for each persona.

Marketing Manager: "When I'm building a new campaign, I want AI to suggest optimal send times and subject lines based on past performance, so I can launch faster without second-guessing every decision."
→ The AI isn't the point — reduced decision fatigue is. Functional: speed. Emotional: confidence.

VP of Marketing: "When I'm justifying marketing spend to the CFO, I want AI-powered attribution that connects campaigns to revenue, so I can prove ROI and secure next quarter's budget."
→ They don't care about the AI — they care about the story the data tells. Functional: attribution. Social: credibility with C-suite.

Marketing Ops Admin: "When I'm onboarding a new tool, I want the AI to require minimal configuration and not break existing workflows, so I can avoid a painful migration and keep the team productive."
→ For this persona, "better AI" might be a negative — more complexity, more things to configure and debug. Functional: low maintenance. Emotional: no anxiety.

The conflict: The VP wants sophisticated AI to impress the C-suite. The Admin wants simplicity. The Manager just wants to move faster. "Better AI features" is a solution — the jobs are all different. Your product strategy needs to address all three, and the Admin's anxiety is often the silent deal-killer that sales never surfaces.

Meeting Move of the Day

When a stakeholder describes what they want built, ask:

"When does this need arise — what's the situation the user is in right before they'd reach for this?"

This is the JTBD circumstance question. It's powerful because it forces the conversation to ground the feature in a real moment, not an abstract capability. "We need better analytics" is vague. "On Monday morning, when I'm trying to decide which campaigns to pause before the weekly standup" is a spec you can design from.

Building Your Toolkit
Day 1: "What user behavior do we expect to change?" (User Lens)
Day 2: "What problem did they build that to solve?" (First Principles)
Day 3: "When does this need arise?" (JTBD Circumstance)

Three meetings, three different questions. Each one pulls the conversation out of solution-space and into problem-space from a different angle.
Glossary
Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)
A framework that describes the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. People "hire" products to do jobs — features are how, jobs are why.
Functional / Emotional / Social Jobs
Three layers of every job: what needs to get done (functional), how the person wants to feel (emotional), and how they want to be perceived (social). B2B buyers respond to all three.
Four Forces of Progress
Push (current pain) + Pull (new solution's appeal) vs Anxiety (switching fear) + Habit (status quo comfort). Switching happens when the first pair outweighs the second.
Competing Against Non-Consumption
When your biggest competitor isn't another product but "doing nothing." The prospect uses spreadsheets, email, or manual processes. The job exists — they just haven't hired anyone for it yet.
Circumstance
The specific situation that triggers a job. In JTBD, context determines what "good" looks like — the same person has different jobs in different circumstances.
