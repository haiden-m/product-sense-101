WEEK 4 · PRODUCT SENSE IN PRACTICE DAY 16 OF 30

Product Critique

Three weeks of frameworks have given you a vocabulary for decisions you can't always see firsthand — JTBD, moats, RICE, debt quadrants. Today turns that same discipline on something you look at every week: a mock, a competitor's screen, a flow in review. The skill isn't having better taste. It's refusing to start there.

Yesterday you learned to classify technical debt by two questions — was it deliberate, and was it prudent — instead of reacting to how risky a shortcut felt. Product critique asks you to apply that same habit to something far more visible and far more tempting to react to on instinct: a design review where everyone in the room has an opinion about the button color within four seconds, and the actual launch blocker is still sitting three questions downstream, unasked.
01

The Big Idea

"I like it" and "I don't love this" are reactions, not critiques. A critique is a structured evaluation of a design decision against a goal, specific enough that someone could act on it and falsifiable enough that someone could disagree with it on the merits. The gap between the two is most of what separates a junior reviewer from a senior one in a design review — not expertise in visual design, but discipline about what to evaluate first.

Untrained critique starts at the top of what's visible: the color, the icon, the copy tone, the spacing — call this layer Craft. It's the first thing anyone notices, so it's the first thing anyone comments on, and a review can burn its entire fifteen minutes there without ever touching whether the thing actually works. Senior critique runs the opposite direction — bottom-up, through four layers, cheapest and most load-bearing first:

4
Craft

Visual polish, tone, micro-interaction, delight. Real, and worth getting right — but it's a coat of paint on whatever the first three layers decided, so it's reviewed last, not first.

3
Consequence

What happens downstream when this works as designed, and separately, what happens when the underlying logic is wrong. Second-order effects on adjacent data, other teams' workflows, and edge cases nobody in the room is picturing.

2
Consistency

Does this match the patterns the product has already taught the user elsewhere? A locally clever solution that contradicts an established pattern creates a tax the user pays every time they switch screens.

1
Clarity

Can the user tell what this does, why it's here, and what happens if they interact with it — without reading a tooltip or asking a colleague? If the answer's no, nothing above this layer matters yet.

REVIEW IN THIS ORDER — BOTTOM TO TOP

Call this the Critique Stack. The order matters because each layer gates the ones above it: polishing the craft on a screen with a clarity problem is finishing a room nobody can find the door into. And the layers don't cost the same to fix at each stage — a clarity gap caught in a design review is a copy edit; the same gap caught after launch is a support queue and a re-onboarding email to every customer who got confused.

Taste is not disqualifying — it's just layer 4

Having a strong aesthetic point of view is a real skill, and nothing here argues against it. The discipline is sequencing: say what you notice about craft, but ask first whether the thing is clear, consistent, and safe when it's wrong. A reviewer who can only critique craft is a stakeholder, not a product reviewer — the product sense is in knowing which layer you're actually standing on when you open your mouth.

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

A design review runs fifteen minutes on a new "AI Suggested Filter" button added to the workflow editor — and almost ships a data-loss bug because the whole room critiqued the same layer.

"Love the sparkle icon, but can we make the button teal instead of purple to match the rest of the builder? Also 'Suggested Segment' feels a little flat — what about 'Smart Segment'?"

Icon choice, button color, copy tone ("Suggested" vs. "Smart")4 — CraftNo — real, but reversible in a five-minute follow-up ticket

The reframe: the silent-overwrite issue wasn't hidden — it was sitting in the second screen of the prototype the whole time. It got raised in the last ninety seconds of the review, after the craft conversation had run its course, by the one PM who happened to click through instead of just looking at the first frame. For an enterprise customer running compliance-sensitive campaigns, a segment definition silently replaced with no audit trail isn't a rough edge, it's the kind of incident that costs a renewal. Reviewing bottom-up isn't slower — it would have surfaced the real issue in the first three minutes instead of the last one.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation A junior PM on your team shares a mock for "Smart Send-Time," a new feature that lets the system auto-pick each recipient's send time instead of the marketer setting one manually. The review is in fifteen minutes. The mock shows a clean toggle: "Let Smart Send-Time optimize delivery for this campaign," off by default. The junior PM asks what you think before the meeting. What do you actually look at first, and what do you tell them?

Step 1 — Clarity: does the toggle say what it does?

  • "Optimize delivery" is a craft-adjacent clarity gap dressed up as a benefit statement — it doesn't tell the marketer when their campaign will actually go out, which matters enormously for a time-sensitive promotion. A marketer flipping this on has no way to picture the outcome. That's worth fixing, but it's not where the fifteen minutes should start.

Step 2 — Consistency: does this match how the builder already handles timing?

  • Every other scheduling control in the builder shows an explicit date and time the marketer set themselves. This is the first control that hands timing to the system. That's a legitimate design decision, but it's a pattern break the team should make on purpose, not discover after a confused support ticket.

Step 3 — Consequence: what happens when the system's guess is wrong?

  • This is the question to ask before the meeting, not during it: what does the marketer see if Smart Send-Time pushes delivery outside a compliance-mandated quiet-hours window in a regulated region, or past a hard campaign deadline the marketer didn't realize the toggle could override? If there's no visible preview of the computed send window before the campaign launches, this is a repeat of yesterday's debt-quadrant trap — a shortcut nobody named as a risk, shipped because the happy path looked clean.

Step 4 — What you tell the junior PM before the meeting

  • "The toggle and copy are a five-minute fix, so don't lead the review with those. Lead with one question: show me what the marketer sees, for this specific campaign, before they hit send — and if the answer is 'nothing, they just trust it,' that's the conversation to have with engineering today, not the thing we quietly find out from a customer next month."

The trap this drill is testing

  • The mock is clean, the toggle is simple, and simple-looking features are exactly the ones whose consequence layer goes unexamined — there's no complexity on screen to prompt the question. Critique discipline matters most precisely when there's nothing visually alarming to trigger it.
MEETING MOVE

Use this to open a design review

Before anyone comments on how a mock looks, redirect the room with:

"Before we talk about how this looks — can we walk through what happens when the system's guess is wrong, and is that visible to the user before they commit to anything?"
05

Glossary

Product Critique
A structured evaluation of a design decision against its intended goal — specific and falsifiable — as distinct from an unstructured reaction like "I like it" or "something feels off."
The Critique Stack
A bottom-up review order — Clarity, Consistency, Consequence, Craft — that catches load-bearing issues before the conversation settles on visual polish. Each layer gates the ones above it.
Heuristic Evaluation
Reviewing a design against a known set of usability principles (Nielsen's ten heuristics are the classic reference) rather than against personal preference or a live user test. Gives critique a shared standard to argue from.
Second-Order Effect
A consequence one step removed from the obvious, direct one — not "does the button work" but "what happens to adjacent systems, other teams, or edge-case users when it does exactly what it's designed to do, or exactly the opposite."
"I Like / I Wish / What If"
A design-studio critique format that keeps feedback generative instead of purely evaluative: name what's working, name a concern as a wish rather than a verdict, then offer a concrete alternative to react to.
