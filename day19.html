North Star Metrics

Yesterday you learned that a true number can still point you at the wrong decision. Today's framework is how you pick the one number your whole org steers by — and how you stop that same number from becoming the next thing quietly lying to you.

Yesterday you got the test for reading a single metric: is it the decision, or one input into it. A North Star Metric is where that test gets applied at the highest stakes in the company — it's the one number leadership rallies the whole org around, which means it's also the one number everyone has the most incentive to move by any means available. Goodhart's Law, which showed up yesterday almost in passing, is the whole reason today's lesson exists.
01

The Big Idea

A North Star Metric (NSM) is the single metric that best captures the value your product delivers to customers right now — chosen so that moving it reliably predicts the business outcomes you actually care about, like retention and revenue, months before those lagging numbers confirm it.

A good NSM passes four tests at once. Get any one wrong and the metric stops working as a compass, even though it still looks fine on a dashboard.

  • Reflects customer value, not company convenience. It should go up when customers are genuinely getting more value, not when your team ships more of something.
  • Leads, rather than lags. It should move early enough that a team can still act on it — revenue and churn confirm the story weeks or quarters after it was already decided.
  • Is actionable by the teams who own it. Someone building a feature this sprint should be able to point at a lever that moves this number, not just hope it improves.
  • Is resistant to being gamed alone. No NSM is un-gameable on its own — which is exactly why it never travels without a counter-metric.
— North Star, Unguarded "Move the number. Nothing else is being watched."

One metric, tracked alone and tied to goals, bonuses, or a board narrative. Whatever moves it fastest — genuine value or a cheap trick — looks identical on the dashboard.

What happens: the team finds the cheapest path to the number, because that's what every incentive in the room is pointing at.

+ North Star, Guarded "Move the number — and show the counter-metric didn't move against us."

The NSM is reported next to one or two counter-metrics chosen specifically to catch its most likely failure mode, so a cheap win shows up as a win on one line and a loss on the other.

What happens: the only path to a win on both lines is the one that was actually supposed to count.

This is yesterday's Goodhart's Law, made structural

Day 18 defined Goodhart's Law as what happens once a measure becomes a target: people start optimizing the measure instead of the goal it stood in for. A North Star Metric is a measure that gets made into a target on purpose, company-wide, usually with comp or a board narrative attached. That's not a reason to avoid having one — it's the reason the counter-metric isn't optional. The counter-metric is Goodhart's Law priced into the system from day one, instead of discovered the hard way in a quarter where the number went up and the business quietly got worse.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

Your product adopts Weekly Active Workflows — automations actually running and delivering value to end users — as its North Star. It's a good candidate: it reflects real usage of the platform's core value, and it's a leading indicator of renewal. One team, measured on growing it, ships a feature that auto-duplicates a client's existing workflow across every untouched account segment, with one click. The number jumps. Nobody checked what else moved.

The reframe: every one of these numbers is accurate. The North Star climbed exactly as designed. What was missing wasn't better data — it was a counter-metric, engagement rate per campaign, reported on the same slide every week. That single second line would have flagged the problem in week one, instead of waiting for NRR to confirm it two quarters later, once renewal conversations were already underway.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation Leadership wants to lock in Total Marketing Actions Sent — every email, SMS, and push notification sent through your platform — as the company's North Star Metric, company-wide, starting next quarter. It goes to the board as the headline number in two weeks. You have one week to make your case. What do you push back on, and what do you propose instead?

Why this candidate fails the four tests

  • It rewards volume, not customer value. A client can raise this number by sending more messages to the same audience — that's easier than doing anything that actually helps their business, and it's exactly the cheap path the unguarded-NSM pattern predicts.
  • It can move in a direction that actively harms the business. More sends with no corresponding value raises opt-outs and spam complaints, which hurts deliverability for every client on shared sending infrastructure — the metric can go up while the platform's core asset quietly degrades.

What you propose instead

  • A North Star closer to audience segments reaching a defined engagement outcome (opened, clicked, converted) — it only goes up when the send actually worked, not just when it happened.
  • Two counter-metrics reported on the same slide, every time: unsubscribe / spam-complaint rate and 90-day client Net Revenue Retention. The first catches gaming within days; the second catches anything subtler within a quarter.

What you say to leadership with one week left

  • "Total sends can go up while we're quietly burning deliverability and trust — and we won't see it in this number until it shows up in a renewal call. Let's pair whatever we pick with a guardrail metric before the board sees it as the headline, and pilot both for two weeks on a handful of accounts before we lock in company-wide."
MEETING MOVE

Use this whenever a metric is proposed as THE metric

One question that forces the guardrail conversation before anyone locks in a North Star:

"What's the cheapest way someone could move this number this week without creating any of the value it's supposed to represent — and which counter-metric would catch that?"
05

Glossary

North Star Metric (NSM)
The single metric chosen to best capture the core value a product delivers to customers, selected to correlate with long-term business success while staying understandable and actionable across the organization.
Counter-Metric (Guardrail Metric)
A metric tracked alongside the North Star specifically to catch the way it's most likely to be gamed or over-optimized — it moves against you exactly when the North Star is being won the wrong way.
Leading Indicator
A metric that predicts a future outcome early enough to still act on it — the property that makes a metric useful as a North Star rather than just a report card.
Lagging Indicator
A metric that confirms an outcome only after it has already happened — revenue, churn, NRR. Essential for validation, useless for steering week to week.
Metrics Constellation
The small set of input metrics that feed into and explain movement in the North Star — giving individual teams a lever they can actually pull without needing to own the whole business outcome.
