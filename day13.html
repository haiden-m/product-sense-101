The Kano Model

Yesterday answered when to ship. Today answers a question that sits underneath every score you've ever put in a RICE sheet: when you ship it, what shape does the satisfaction actually take?

Yesterday you separated how big an opportunity is from how fast it's leaking value — Cost of Delay gave urgency its own axis instead of burying it inside "impact." Today adds a second axis Cost of Delay doesn't touch: what kind of satisfaction curve does this feature even have? Not every feature converts investment into happiness the same way, and scoring them as if they do is how roadmaps end up overbuilding things customers already stopped noticing.
01

The Big Idea

Noriaki Kano, a Japanese quality researcher, noticed in 1984 that customer satisfaction isn't one straight line from "didn't build it" to "built a lot of it." Different attributes follow completely different curves — and a prioritization framework that treats them all as the same slope will keep misallocating a team's time.

Kano sorted product attributes into three working categories, by plotting how much you've built against how satisfied it makes customers:

Must-be (threshold / table stakes)

Absence causes real anger. Presence causes no credit at all — it just gets you back to neutral. SSO for an enterprise buyer, 99.9% uptime, SOC 2. Nobody ever says "wow, it logged me in correctly." They only ever say something when it doesn't.

Performance (linear / one-dimensional)

Satisfaction tracks investment in a fairly straight line. Faster segment builds, more supported channels, higher send throughput. More genuinely is better, and customers will tell you so directly when you ask.

Attractive (delighter)

Absence isn't missed — nobody was asking for it. Presence creates disproportionate delight relative to the effort it took. An AI that quietly suggests the best send time per recipient, surfaced without being asked.

Must-be Performance Attractive

The trap RICE and Cost of Delay both fall into

Both frameworks score "impact" as a single number, which quietly assumes a straight-line relationship between effort and satisfaction — the Performance curve. Must-be attributes break that assumption on one side: you can't out-satisfy a customer by having "more" uptime than 100%, so investment past the threshold is close to wasted, no matter how high you'd score its "impact." Attractive attributes break it on the other side: a small, clever investment can produce satisfaction wildly out of proportion to its RICE score, because nobody was modeling it as having any floor to clear at all. And every attribute migrates over time — today's delighter is next year's must-be, as competitors copy it and customers start expecting it. The AI send-time suggestion that delights this year is the checkbox item every RFP asks about in three years.

02

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

A B2B SaaS product is scoring three backlog items with the same RICE template, and all three come back with similar total scores.

"They're all roughly an 8 out of 10 on impact. Let's just sequence by effort, cheapest first."

Running a short Kano questionnaire against each — asking customers both a functional question ("how do you feel if this is present?") and a dysfunctional question ("how do you feel if this is absent?") — produces a very different picture:

ItemIf absentIf presentCategory
Enterprise SSOFurious, deal blockedShrug, expectedMust-be
Faster segment buildsMildly annoyedNoticeably happierPerformance
AI send-time suggestionDoesn't noticeDelighted, tells peersAttractive

The reframe: a single "impact" number flattened three completely different jobs into one shape. SSO isn't competing for satisfaction at all — it's a cost of entry, and shipping it doesn't win anything, it just stops losing enterprise deals that are already in motion. The segment-build speedup is the one place "more effort, more satisfaction" genuinely holds, so it's the right candidate for the kind of linear scoring RICE was built for. And the AI suggestion is where a modest build can produce a disproportionate, story-worthy reaction — exactly the kind of bet a flat RICE score would never surface as the standout. Same backlog, same effort estimates, three different reasons to build each one.

Scenario Drill

The situation Your VP of Product just came out of three calls with power-user customers — senior power users who live in the workflow editor every day. All three independently asked for more configuration options: custom field logic, conditional branching, a scripting layer for segment rules. "Our most engaged customers are asking for this by name. Let's prioritize it for next quarter." Support data shows a different story: for the broader base of marketing generalists who make up most of the account list, the builder's current flexibility already generates the single highest volume of support tickets and the lowest onboarding completion rate of any screen in the product. What do you tell the VP?

Step 1 — Notice whose Kano curve you're looking at

  • "Our customers are asking for it" is true, but it's true of one segment. Kano categories aren't universal properties of a feature — they're properties of a feature for a given user segment. More configuration options may be a genuine Performance attribute for power users and an Indifferent-to-Reverse attribute for generalists, who experience additional flexibility as additional ways to get it wrong.

Step 2 — Weigh the evidence you already have, not just the loudest evidence

  • Three calls with your most vocal, most engaged customers is a real signal, but it's a sample that self-selects for people who already push the tool hard. The support-ticket and onboarding data is a signal from the segment that doesn't call you — the quiet majority, whose satisfaction curve this change could push the wrong way if the added complexity lands on the default experience instead of behind an opt-in.

Step 3 — Reframe the decision for the VP

  • "This is a real request, but it's a Performance attribute for maybe 15% of our base and a likely Reverse attribute for the rest — the complexity that would delight power users is the same complexity that's already driving our highest ticket volume for generalists. I don't want to say no to this; I want to scope it as an advanced mode those power users can opt into, instead of a change to the default builder everyone else has to navigate around. Let's size both: the advanced-mode build, and what it would take to fix the onboarding drop-off we already know about."

The trap this drill is testing

  • The dangerous move isn't ignoring customer requests — it's generalizing one segment's Kano curve onto the whole base because that segment happened to be the one in the room. The senior move is asking "attractive or performance for whom, and what does it do to everyone else's curve" before committing the quarter.
MEETING MOVE

Use this in your next roadmap or backlog review

Next time a feature gets scored only on effort and impact, redirect with:

"If we didn't build this, would customers be upset, indifferent, or relieved — and if we tripled the investment, would satisfaction keep climbing or flatten out? Which Kano category are we actually betting on here, and for which segment?"
05

Glossary

Kano Model
A framework, from Noriaki Kano's 1984 research, that classifies product attributes by the shape of the curve between how much is built and how satisfied customers are, rather than by a single linear impact score.
Must-be (Threshold) Attribute
An attribute whose absence causes strong dissatisfaction but whose presence earns no extra credit — it only restores a baseline. Also called table stakes or basic requirements.
Performance (One-Dimensional) Attribute
An attribute where satisfaction rises roughly linearly with how much is built — customers will state this preference directly when asked, and more genuinely is better.
Attractive (Delighter) Attribute
An attribute whose absence isn't noticed or missed, but whose presence produces satisfaction disproportionate to the investment — the surprise nobody asked for.
Feature Migration (Kano Drift)
The tendency of attributes to move down-curve over time — an attractive delighter becomes a performance feature as customers start to expect it, then a must-be once every competitor has matched it.
