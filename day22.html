Asking Sharper Questions

Yesterday you learned to lead every answer with the governing thought. Today's lesson comes before that moment: the question that gets you an answer worth structuring in the first place. A vague question earns a vague answer, and no amount of pyramid structure rescues a draft built on the wrong premise.

Yesterday you learned to put the governing thought first and back it with MECE reasons. That only works if the governing thought is actually true — and whether it's true usually depends on what you asked before you started writing. Today you're building the other half of the skill: a small repertoire of question templates that replace "can you say more?" with questions precise enough to unpack a vague claim before it becomes your governing thought.
01

The Big Idea

Senior PMs aren't the people in the room with the most answers. They're the people whose questions make the room's answers sharper. That's a skill, not a personality trait — and like any skill, it's built from a small set of reusable templates, not improvised fresh every time someone says something vague.

Most PMs default to one question shape: "can you tell me more?" It's polite, it's safe, and it rarely moves the conversation anywhere, because it hands the ambiguity straight back to the person who was already ambiguous. A sharper question doesn't ask for more words — it asks for a specific kind of information that collapses the ambiguity. Five templates cover almost every situation you'll hit in a B2B SaaS PM's week:

Definition "When you say 'underperforming,' underperforming relative to what?"

Unpacks a vague term before you build anything on top of it. Most stakeholder claims hide an unstated benchmark.

Evidence "What have you actually seen that makes you think that?"

Separates an observation from an inference. Half of "the customer wants X" is someone's guess wearing a customer's voice.

Boundary "What would have to be true for this to work?"

Surfaces the assumptions a plan is silently standing on, before you commit engineering time to testing them the expensive way.

Priority "If you could only have one of these two, which, and why?"

Forces a real trade-off into the open. A stakeholder who wants "both, equally" hasn't actually prioritized anything yet.

Counterfactual "What happens if we do nothing?"

Tests urgency honestly. If the answer is "not much," the request was never as urgent as its framing implied.

  • Match the template to the failure mode. A vague term needs a Definition question. An unsupported claim needs an Evidence question. A plan with hidden risk needs a Boundary question. Reaching for the wrong one wastes the question.
  • Ask before you agree. The templates work best in the first pass of a conversation, not as damage control after you've already said "sure, we'll scope that." Once you've nodded along, the sharper question reads as second-guessing instead of diligence.
  • One sharp question beats three soft ones. A single well-aimed Boundary question can replace an entire round of "can you clarify" follow-ups, because it asks for the one fact that actually changes the plan.
  • Silence is data. If a sharp question gets a long pause instead of a crisp answer, that pause is itself the finding — it usually means nobody has tested the assumption you just named.
— The Soft Follow-Up "Got it — can you say a bit more about that?"

Invites the speaker to repeat their claim in different words. It feels collaborative, but it rarely produces new information — the ambiguity just gets rephrased, not resolved.

What happens: two more meetings later, the team is still debating what the original claim actually meant.

+ The Templated Question "What have you seen that makes you think that, specifically?"

Asks for a named kind of information — evidence, a boundary condition, a trade-off — that the speaker either has or doesn't. Either way, you learn something real in one exchange.

What happens: you find out in the same meeting whether the claim rests on a support ticket, a hunch, or one loud customer call.

This is what makes your pyramid honest

Day 21's governing thought is only as good as the claims it's built on. If you skip straight to structuring an answer without first asking a Definition or Evidence question, you risk writing a beautifully organized pyramid on top of someone else's untested assumption. Sharper questions are the quality check that happens before you write a single sentence of structure.

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

Your Sales lead messages: "Big account says they need custom field mapping for their CRM sync, ASAP, or they're at risk." You have a 15-minute window before this becomes a commitment on the roadmap.

The reframe: none of these questions were adversarial. They were specific. "What have you seen that makes you think that?" isn't the PM doubting Sales — it's the PM doing the one thing that prevents a three-sprint-week commitment from being made on the strength of a single forwarded email.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation In a roadmap review, your engineering lead says: "The recommendation engine is underperforming, and I think we need to rebuild the ranking model." The room starts nodding along toward a quarter-long rebuild.
  • No metric has been named yet.
  • Nobody has asked what "rebuild" would cost versus a narrower fix.
  • The last time ranking was touched was two quarters ago, for an unrelated reason.
Before the room commits to a rebuild: which question templates do you reach for, in what order, and what do you actually ask out loud?

The question to avoid asking first

  • "How long would a rebuild take?" — this accepts the solution before the problem has been defined, and commits the room to scoping an answer to a question nobody has actually asked yet.

The order that actually unpacks it

  • Definition, first: "Underperforming relative to what — what metric, and what's the target we're missing?" This alone often reveals there's no agreed metric, which is the real finding.
  • Evidence, second: "What have you seen that points to the ranking model specifically, versus the underlying data or the features feeding it?" A model can look like the problem when the real issue is stale or incomplete training data.
  • Boundary, third: "What would have to be true for a rebuild to actually fix this, that a narrower change wouldn't?" This forces the case for "rebuild" versus "retune" or "re-feed" into the open.

What usually turns up

  • The "underperforming" claim often traces back to one metric dip that coincides with an unrelated data pipeline change, not the ranking logic itself — a Definition and Evidence question pair catches this in minutes, not a quarter.
"Before we scope a rebuild: what metric is the recommendation engine missing, and against what target? And is there anything pointing specifically at the ranking model, versus the data feeding it? If a data or feature issue turns out to be the real driver, that's a two-week fix, not a quarter-long rebuild — worth confirming which one we're actually looking at before we commit the team."
MEETING MOVE

Use this the next time someone hands you an urgent, under-specified ask

Ask it before you agree to anything, not after:

"What have you actually seen that makes you think that — and what happens if we do nothing for two weeks?"
05

Glossary

Definition Question
A question that asks a vague term to name its own benchmark — "underperforming relative to what?" — before anyone builds a plan on top of it.
Evidence Question
A question that separates a direct observation from an inference wearing a customer's or a metric's voice — "what have you actually seen?"
Boundary Question
A question that surfaces the assumptions a plan is silently standing on — "what would have to be true for this to work?" — before the team pays to test them the expensive way.
Priority Question
A question that forces an unresolved trade-off into the open — "if you could only have one, which, and why?" — instead of letting "both, equally" stand as an answer.
Counterfactual Question
A question that tests whether an urgent framing is actually urgent — "what happens if we do nothing?" — by naming the cost of inaction explicitly.
