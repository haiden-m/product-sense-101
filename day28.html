API & Platform PM

Building for developers and ecosystems — where your customer stops being a person and becomes another piece of software, and the product is a promise you can't quietly take back.

The Big Idea

Every lesson so far has assumed your customer is a human looking at a screen. Platform PM breaks that assumption. When you ship an API, a webhook, or an SDK, your customer is a developer, and your product surface is a contract written in request shapes, status codes, and field names instead of buttons and copy.

That shift sounds technical, but it's really a product shift. You're no longer designing an experience someone moves through once; you're designing an interface someone else's code will call thousands of times, often for years, often without ever upgrading. Back on Day 9 you learned that a platform's value compounds when other people build on top of it — API & Platform PM is the discipline of actually earning that.

User Lens → Developer Experience
The "user" is reading your docs at 11pm trying to ship their own deadline. Confusing errors and inconsistent naming are usability bugs, same as a confusing button.
Business Lens → Platform economics
Every integration a partner builds is distribution you didn't pay for — but every integration you support is also a liability you now carry indefinitely.
Technical Lens → The contract
An API is a promise. Once a third party depends on a field or a behavior, "fixing" it is a breaking change to someone else's production system.
Market Lens → Ecosystem moat
A platform that attracts partners who build switching-cost-raising integrations is defensible in a way a single great feature never is.

The practical tool for a platform PM is the Platform Test — three questions that decide whether a capability should be built as a one-off feature or exposed as a durable platform primitive:

1. Will more than one consumer need this? A one-off integration serves one customer. A primitive serves every customer who will ever ask for something like it.

2. Can we expose it as a contract we're willing to keep? If the underlying data model is still churning internally, it's too early to promise external stability.

3. Does opening it build a moat, or does it commoditize us? Exposing your scheduling engine might help partners; exposing your scoring model might hand competitors your IP.
The Technical PM Trap, platform edition. A technical background makes it easy to fall in love with the elegance of the API itself — clean REST design, perfect idempotency keys, a beautiful SDK. None of that is the product. The product is whether a developer at a partner company can accomplish their job faster with your platform than by building it themselves. An elegant API nobody adopts is a well-built feature nobody asked for, wearing a different costume.
The B2B SaaS Angle

In API-first B2B products, platform thinking shows up constantly. Customer data flows in from a dozen source systems; event triggers need to fire the moment a record crosses a threshold; agencies and systems integrators want to build on top of your orchestration engine rather than wait for your roadmap.

Two patterns recur in this domain specifically. First, event delivery: should a real-time customer-state change reach a partner system via webhook (you push, low latency, but you now own retry and delivery guarantees) or via a polling endpoint (they pull, simpler for you, laggier for them)? Second, data gravity: once a partner's workflow depends on reading customer segments from your platform, they're unlikely to rebuild that logic elsewhere — which is exactly the switching cost that makes platform investment worth it, provided you don't break the contract underneath them.

The enterprise buyer/user split from Day 1 gets a third party added here: the partner developer, who never buys anything but decides, by how painful your API is, whether the integration that was supposed to lock in your buyer actually ships.

Worked Example

The Loyalty-Tier Webhook Request

Your largest customer's BI team wants to know the instant a shopper's loyalty tier changes, so their internal dashboard stays in sync. Three ways to respond land on your desk:

A Write a one-off nightly export script that dumps tier changes to their S3 bucket
B Tell them to poll your existing customer-profile API every few minutes and diff the tier field themselves
C Build a generic Customer Event Webhook framework — tier change is the first of many event types it can emit

The product sense read: A solves today's ticket and creates tomorrow's maintenance burden — a bespoke script that only one engineer understands, with no monitoring and no reuse. B pushes real cost onto the partner (polling delay, wasted calls, diffing logic they shouldn't have to write) in exchange for zero engineering effort on your side — cheap for you, expensive for the ecosystem.

C passes the Platform Test: more than one consumer will want event notifications (segment entry, churn-risk flag, consent change — tier is just the first), the event envelope can be a contract you design deliberately from day one instead of retrofitting later, and a general event system is a capability competitors would have to build from scratch to match. The 6-week estimate for C looks worse than A's 3 days — until you count the fourth customer who asks for the same thing next quarter.

Quiz · Test Your Thinking
1 Your API currently returns a customer's status as a free-text string like "Active" or "active" depending on which internal service wrote it. A partner's integration breaks whenever the casing changes. What's the real product failure here?
A The partner should have written more defensive code
B String fields are inherently unreliable and should be avoided
C The API shipped without a stable contract — an enum with defined values the team is committed to, instead of an implementation detail leaking through
D This is purely an engineering QA issue, not a product one
Correct: C. This is the Technical Lens applied to platform work: an API's job is to expose a deliberate contract, not whatever shape the internal data happens to be in today. A platform PM defines the enum, documents every valid value, and treats a casing change as a breaking change requiring a version bump — because somewhere, a partner's production system is parsing that exact string.
2 A partner asks for a webhook instead of having to poll your API every minute. What's the main product trade-off you're accepting by saying yes?
A Webhooks are always cheaper to build than polling endpoints
B You take on responsibility for retry logic, delivery guarantees, and ordering — failures now happen on your infrastructure instead of theirs
C Webhooks eliminate the need for authentication
D There is no meaningful difference from the partner's side
Correct: B. Polling puts the burden of checking on the partner; a webhook moves that burden onto you. You now own what happens when their endpoint is down, whether events can arrive out of order, and whether a dropped event is silently lost. The Business Lens question is whether that ongoing operational cost is worth the distribution and stickiness the webhook buys you.
3 Six months after launch, your platform API has three large partners depending on a field you now realize is badly designed. What does "backward compatibility" require of you?
A Keep the old field working as documented, and introduce the improved version alongside it with a migration path and a deprecation timeline
B Fix the field immediately — partners should adapt quickly to improvements
C Email partners once and change the field a week later
D Wait for a partner to complain before doing anything
Correct: A. Backward compatibility means a published contract keeps behaving the way its consumers were told it would, even after you've learned better. The discipline is additive change plus a deprecation window, not silent mutation. This is the cost of calling something a platform rather than a feature — you've sold stability, and reneging on it is more expensive than it looks from inside the building.
Scenario Drill

Sales just promised a strategic prospect "real-time API access to segment membership changes" to close a deal that renews in 6 weeks. Engineering says the existing segment engine has no event hooks at all — building generic ones is a 5-week project; faking it for this one customer with a scheduled poll-and-diff job is 4 days.

Apply all four lenses before deciding. Use the prompts below:

User Lens What does "real-time" actually need to mean for the workflow the prospect described — seconds, minutes, same business day?
Business Lens What's the cost of under-delivering on a sales promise versus the cost of 5 extra weeks of engineering time?
Technical Lens Does the quick fix become throwaway code, or can it be built as a thin first version of the real thing?
Market Lens Is "real-time segment events" a capability your competitors already offer, or a genuine differentiator worth owning properly?
No wrong answers — this is about practicing the lens discipline.

A strong answer touches all four:

User: Before building anything, get the actual requirement from the prospect's team, not the sales summary. "Real-time" from sales often means "not a weekly CSV export" — a 5-minute poll interval may fully satisfy the real workflow, which changes the engineering estimate dramatically.

Business: A broken promise to a strategic prospect at signing is expensive in trust, not just revenue — but so is burning 5 weeks of the platform roadmap for one logo if the capability doesn't generalize. The real question is whether this deal is large enough, or representative enough of the pipeline, to justify accelerating a platform investment you'd eventually make anyway.

Technical: The deciding move is making the 4-day version a deliberate V1 of the real architecture rather than a disposable hack — same event schema, same webhook contract, just backed by a poll-and-diff job internally at first. That way the "shortcut" is forward-compatible, and swapping in true event hooks later is invisible to the partner.

Market: If real-time event webhooks are becoming table stakes in B2B platforms, this isn't really a one-customer favor — it's a deferred platform investment that a sales deal just forced onto the roadmap. Treat the deadline as a forcing function, not a distraction from it.

The decision isn't "ship the hack" versus "build it right" — it's building the right contract first, behind whichever implementation the timeline allows.

Meeting Move of the Day

Next time a one-off integration or custom API request comes up in a meeting, ask:

"If the fifth partner asked for this exact same thing, what would we wish we'd built today?"

This question forces the room past the immediate requester and into platform thinking without you having to say the word "platform." It surfaces whether you're looking at a one-off favor or the first instance of a pattern — and it's far more persuasive than arguing in the abstract about technical debt.

Practice it once this week. Notice who in the room starts thinking in contracts instead of tickets.

Glossary
API-First Design
Designing the API contract before building the internal implementation or any UI on top of it, so the interface reflects deliberate product decisions rather than whatever the internals happen to expose.
Developer Experience (DX)
The UX discipline applied to developers as users — clear docs, predictable naming, useful error messages, and a sandbox to test against. Bad DX is a usability bug, full stop.
Backward Compatibility
A guarantee that existing integrations keep working after you ship a change. Breaking it silently is the platform equivalent of changing a signed contract without telling the other party.
Webhook vs. Polling
A webhook pushes data to a partner the moment an event happens (low latency, but you own delivery and retries); polling has the partner pull on a schedule (simpler for you, laggier and chattier for them).
Platform Moat
The defensibility a platform earns when partners build integrations on top of it — switching away means rebuilding someone else's work, not just replacing a feature. Builds directly on Day 9's network effects and Day 7's moats.
