Market Sizing: TAM, SAM, SOM

Yesterday you learned to test whether an advantage compounds before calling it a moat. Today's framework asks the question that has to come first: is the market behind that advantage even big enough to justify building it — and how much of it can you actually reach?

01 The Big Idea 5 min read

A market size isn't one number — it's three, and each one answers a different question

TAM tells you if the category is worth being in at all. SAM tells you what your business model can actually reach. SOM tells you what you can realistically win given who else is competing for it. Conflating them is how a genuinely huge market turns into a genuinely disappointing quarter.

Every PM has sat through a slide that says "the market is $80B" as if that settles the roadmap debate. It doesn't, because $80B usually describes the Total Addressable Market — every dollar spent on the category worldwide, including segments your product will never sell into. The number that should drive a roadmap decision is much narrower, and getting from TAM down to something actionable is the actual skill.

TAM — Total Addressable Market Every dollar spent globally on the category, regardless of who could realistically sell it or buy it.
All spend
SAM — Serviceable Addressable Market The slice your business model, geography, deployment, and ICP can actually serve today.
Reachable
SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market What you can realistically capture in a defined window, given sales capacity and competition.
Winnable

The narrowing at each step isn't a rounding error — it's where the real constraints live. TAM → SAM usually cuts on things like: does the buyer need enterprise-grade compliance you don't have yet, is the deployment model (on-prem vs. cloud) one you support, does the segment run on a channel (retail, gaming, telecom) your ICP work hasn't validated. SAM → SOM cuts on capacity: how many net-new logos can your sales and CS org actually onboard in a year, and what share of a competitive deal do you realistically win against incumbents. A PM who only ever quotes TAM is skipping both of those conversations — which is exactly why an investment committee's first question is almost always "SAM, not TAM."

The other reason to run this exercise isn't to produce a defensible number for a slide — it's to force the assumptions behind the number into the open. A bottom-up SAM (count actual target accounts × realistic price) and a top-down SAM (industry report × your addressable slice) rarely match on the first pass, and reconciling why is where you find out whether your ICP is too narrow, too broad, or priced wrong.

02 Worked Example 3 min

Sizing an AI drafting add-on for a B2B workflow product

The Situation

Your team is evaluating whether to build an AI-assisted first-draft generator as a paid add-on to your core product. Before scoping it, you're asked to size the opportunity — bottom-up, using your own install base and pricing, rather than quoting a generic "AI-assisted productivity" industry report.

Global spend on B2B workflow-software market (industry estimate)$28B / yr
↓ narrow to accounts your platform can serve (mid-market/enterprise, cloud-native, English + core EU languages)
Addressable accounts across those segments~42,000 accounts
× realistic attach rate for a new paid add-on in year one (based on comparable add-on launches)
Accounts likely to adopt in year one~4,600 accounts
× add-on price per account per year$3,600
Bottom-up SOM, year one≈ $16.6M

Where each number actually came from: the $28B is TAM — pulled straight from an analyst report, useful only for "is this category worth being in." The 42,000 accounts is SAM, built bottom-up from your own CRM: current customer count in mid-market/enterprise tiers, filtered to the languages and regions your model actually supports today. The 4,600-account adoption estimate is SOM, and it's the number that should carry the most scrutiny — it's not "how big is the opportunity," it's "how many of our own existing customers will actually buy this in twelve months," disciplined by attach rates from the last two add-ons you shipped, not by optimism.

→

The senior PM read: the number that goes in the roadmap decision is the $16.6M SOM, not the $28B TAM — and even that number is only as good as the attach-rate assumption underneath it. The right follow-up isn't "is $16.6M big enough," it's "what would make the attach rate wrong by 2x in either direction" — because that assumption, not the market size, is what the investment case actually rests on.

04 Scenario Drill 8 min

Work through this before revealing the analysis

Your situation

Your CEO wants a market-sizing slide for the board on entering healthcare operations as a new vertical. He's seen a report saying "operations software for mid-market healthcare is a $6B market" and wants to know if it's worth a dedicated go-to-market motion next year. You have three weeks and access to your own CRM (current customers, deal data, win/loss notes) but no budget for custom market research.

Before you reveal the analysis: how would you structure the sizing exercise with what you actually have access to, and what would you tell the board the $6B number does and doesn't tell them?

Name what the $6B actually is, out loud, before building on it

That figure is TAM — global spend across every company doing anything resembling CRM or retention work for gaming, including tools your platform has no plausible path to sell (native game-engine SDKs, ad-network retargeting, console-specific analytics). Presenting it unqualified invites the board to anchor on a number three layers removed from the actual decision. The first job is relabeling it correctly, not disproving it.

Build SAM bottom-up from what you already have

Pull two things from your own CRM: how many gaming-industry leads or inbound trials have touched your pipeline in the past two years (even unconverted ones), and how many of your existing mid-market/enterprise customers run any gaming-adjacent vertical (mobile gaming, live-ops) today. Cross-reference against your platform's actual constraints — does it support the messaging channels gaming CRM typically needs (in-app, push at gaming scale), and does pricing work at gaming's typical margin structure. That gives a defensible "addressable accounts" count without new research spend.

Size SOM from win/loss data, not optimism

Your win/loss notes are the highest-signal asset you have for free: how many gaming deals have you actually competed for, what % you won, and why the losses were lost (feature gap, price, incumbent relationship). If you've won zero gaming deals in two years despite inbound interest, that's a SOM-suppressing signal worth surfacing directly — it says the gap isn't awareness, it's product-market fit for that vertical specifically.

What you'd bring back to the board

"The category is real and roughly $6B globally, but our serviceable slice — accounts we can sell to and support today — is a small fraction of that, and our actual obtainable share based on two years of competitive deal data is smaller still. I'd recommend a scoped pilot (3-5 target accounts, using our existing win/loss learnings to pick winnable ones) before a dedicated GTM motion, rather than sizing the investment off the TAM headline." That reframes the ask from "commit to a vertical" to "fund a cheap test of the one assumption — can we actually win these deals — that the whole case depends on."

05 Meeting Move 2 min

Bring this to your next opportunity review

"That's the TAM — what's our bottom-up SAM using our own account list, and what does our actual win rate in adjacent deals suggest the real SOM is?"

Use it when: a market-size figure is being used to justify an investment decision on its own. It forces the conversation from a single top-line number down to the two numbers — reachable and winnable — that the roadmap decision should actually rest on.

06 Glossary 2 min

Say it like a FAANG PM

TAM Total Addressable Market — total global spend on the category if you captured every possible dollar, regardless of your business model's ability to serve it."TAM tells us the category's worth being in — it doesn't tell us what we can build for it."
SAM Serviceable Addressable Market — the slice of TAM your product, pricing, geography, and ICP can actually serve today."Our SAM excludes on-prem deployments — we don't support that yet."
SOM Serviceable Obtainable Market — what you can realistically capture within a defined time window, constrained by sales capacity and competitive win rate."SOM is the number that should size the investment, not SAM."
Top-down sizing Estimating market size starting from an industry-wide figure (an analyst report) and narrowing it by an estimated addressable percentage.Fast to produce, but only as credible as the percentage narrowing it — often a guess.
Bottom-up sizing Estimating market size by counting actual addressable units (accounts, users) and multiplying by your real price or usage — built from your own data rather than a report."Bottom-up SAM from our CRM came in at a third of the top-down estimate — worth reconciling why."
