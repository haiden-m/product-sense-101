Stakeholder Management

Yesterday's templates get you a sharp answer to a vague claim. But a sharp answer still has to land on someone who can act on it — and in a cross-functional B2B SaaS org, that person rarely reports to you. Today's lesson is about naming who actually owns the decision, and moving people who don't take direction from your title.

Yesterday you built five question templates for unpacking a vague or urgent claim before you agree to anything. That skill assumes you're in the room with the right people. Today addresses what happens when you're not — when a decision needs sign-off from Legal, Security, Sales, and Engineering, none of whom you manage, and no one has said out loud who's actually in charge of saying yes.
01

The Big Idea

Almost none of the authority a senior PM exercises comes from a reporting line. You don't manage Legal, Security, Support, or Sales — yet a typical B2B SaaS launch needs all four to move, on your timeline, without you ever being able to tell any of them what to do. Stakeholder management is the discipline that replaces "I'll just ask nicely and hope" with a repeatable structure for who decides what, and a set of moves for actually influencing people who owe you nothing.

The structural half of that discipline is RACI — a way of naming, for any decision or deliverable, exactly four roles: who's Responsible for doing the work, who's Accountable for the outcome, who's Consulted before the decision is made, and who's merely Informed after it is. Most stalled cross-functional work in B2B SaaS doesn't fail because the plan was bad — it fails because nobody assigned a single, named Accountable, so every function treats itself as equally entitled to weigh in, and the decision drifts until it quietly misses its date.

Responsible Does the work

Can be several people at once — engineers writing the code, legal counsel drafting the clause. Sharing the "R" is normal and often necessary.

Accountable Owns whether it happens

Exactly one person per decision. The moment two people are both Accountable for the same call, neither is fully on the hook, and the decision stalls.

Consulted Asked before the decision

A two-way exchange with enough lead time to actually shape the outcome — not a courtesy ping sent the day before it ships.

Informed Told after the decision

One-way, after the fact. Treating a Consulted stakeholder like an Informed one is the single most common way a sign-off turns into a blocker.

  • RACI is a staffing tool, not a courtesy list. Assign one Accountable per decision, even when three other functions are doing real work on it. If you can't name the one person who answers for the outcome, the decision doesn't have an owner yet.
  • "Consulted" is a promise, not a CC line. Someone marked Consulted gets asked early enough to actually change the plan — not notified the week before launch and expected to be fine with it.
  • Influence rides on what you've synthesized, not what you can command. The PM who walks in having already asked the sharp Evidence and Boundary questions from Day 22 has more leverage in the room than the one who just has the title "Product Manager" on their badge.
  • Match the ask to the stakeholder's own incentive. "This unblocks your Q4 renewal number" moves a Sales leader faster than "this is good for the product" ever will — because it is, from where they sit, actually true and actually theirs.
— Everyone's Invited "Loop in Legal, Security, and Support — let's all align."

No one is named as the person who can actually say yes. Every function treats its input as equally weighted, so the thread grows, the meeting grows, and the decision has nowhere to land.

What happens: three weeks later, the same four people are still "aligning," and the launch date has quietly slipped twice.

+ One Name on the Decision "[Name] is Accountable for the ship call. Legal and Security are Consulted, with sign-off due Thursday."

One person owns the outcome and is empowered to decide once the Consulted functions have weighed in. Everyone else knows exactly what's expected of them and by when.

What happens: the decision gets made in a single meeting, because there's a person in the room whose job it is to make it.

This is what gives your case somewhere to land

Day 21 taught you to lead with the governing thought. Day 22 taught you to pressure-test the claims underneath it. Neither one matters if the room you're presenting to has no named Accountable — a perfectly structured, well-evidenced case can still drift for weeks with no one empowered to act on it. RACI is how you make sure the work from the last two days actually reaches a person who can say yes.

Worked Example

B2B SaaS Scenario

Your team built "Smart Send Time," a feature that times campaign sends per-recipient. It's been code-complete for three weeks. Legal, Security, Support, and Marketing have all been "looped in" the whole time — and the launch still hasn't happened, because no one has been willing to say "ship it" without everyone else nodding first.

Who can actually say "we ship Friday"No one — four functions weighing in with equal, undefined authorityVP Engineering named Accountable; the call gets made in one meeting
Legal's data-handling sign-offThree weeks of follow-up emails nobody owned chasingLogged as Consulted with a 48-hour response SLA; a missed SLA becomes visible immediately
Support's training materialsDiscovered missing the Monday before launchMarked Informed on a fixed trigger date, two weeks ahead of launch

The PM's role in the processIndividually chasing four functions for statusRunning one decision meeting with named owners and dates

The reframe: nothing about the underlying work changed. Legal still had to review the same data-handling question; Support still needed the same training window. What changed is that exactly one person was named Accountable for the ship decision, and everyone else's role — Consulted or Informed — came with a deadline instead of an open invitation to keep weighing in.

04

Scenario Drill

The situation You're launching a data-residency feature required to close a $2M EU renewal. You have authority over none of the people involved.
  • Legal wants a full compliance review — estimated three weeks.
  • Security wants to run a separate audit — estimated two weeks, could run in parallel with Legal's review.
  • Sales Engineering wants early access to demo on this Friday's renewal call.
  • Your own engineering lead says the feature is code-complete, but "nobody's told me who actually signs off."
How do you assign RACI for this launch, and what's the one influence move you make before the next cross-functional sync?

The assignment to avoid

  • Marking yourself Accountable for Legal's or Security's sign-off. You don't have the authority to actually make that call, so doing this just creates false ownership — you'll be the one holding the blame when the real decision-maker overrides you later, having never actually been consulted on your terms.

RACI that matches the authority actually in the room

  • Accountable: whoever already owns the compliance risk if this goes wrong — most likely the Head of Security or a VP of Engineering, not the PM.
  • Responsible: the feature engineers (the code), Legal counsel (the review itself), the Security team (the audit).
  • Consulted: Sales Engineering — asked what Friday's call actually needs before you promise them anything, not after.
  • Informed: the account team, once a real timeline exists to tell them.

The influence move

  • Since Legal and Security don't report to you, the lever isn't urgency — everyone already claims urgency. It's specificity: bring the Head of Security a scoped, concrete ask ("can your two-week audit start today, in parallel with Legal's review, given the renewal closes on the 2nd?") instead of a vague "can we move faster." A named date and a parallel-path option are something they can actually say yes to.
"Legal's review and Security's audit don't have to be sequential — could the audit start today, in parallel, given the renewal closes on the 2nd? And for Friday's call, can Sales Engineering demo in a sandboxed environment rather than production, so we're not promising a ship date before the review and audit are both done?"
MEETING MOVE

Use this in any kickoff or status meeting where ownership is unclear

Ask it before the meeting ends, not in a follow-up email:

"Who is the one person accountable for saying yes or no on this, and by when?"
05

Glossary

RACI Matrix
A table assigning, for each decision or deliverable, who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed — the grammar of decision rights, not a status report.
Accountable (the "A")
The single person who owns whether an outcome happens and answers for it if it doesn't. RACI breaks the moment more than one person holds this for the same decision.
Consulted
Someone whose input is sought before a decision, with enough lead time to actually shape it — treating a Consulted stakeholder like an Informed one is how sign-offs turn into blockers.
Influence Without Authority
Moving a decision forward across a function you don't manage, by trading on shared incentives and synthesized information instead of positional power.
Decision Owner
The person in the room empowered to say yes or no right now. Naming one out loud is often the single highest-leverage move available in a stalled cross-functional meeting.
