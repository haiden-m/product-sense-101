Porter's Five Forces for SaaS

Yesterday you learned to hold off on solutions until the problem was diagnosed. Today's lens zooms out one more level: even the right solution can sit in a bad neighborhood. Five Forces tells you how much of the value you create you'll actually get to keep.

01 The Big Idea 5 min read

Why "we built a great product" isn't the whole story

A feature can be well-built, well-loved by users, and still generate no durable value — because the market structure around it lets someone else capture the upside.

Michael Porter's framing, adapted for B2B SaaS: your product doesn't compete in a vacuum. Five structural pressures decide how much of the value you create you get to keep as revenue, margin, and retention — versus how much leaks away to competitors, customers, vendors, or new challengers. Product sense means reading these forces before you commit a roadmap quarter, not after a renewal call goes sideways.

1 Competitive Rivalry How many vendors chase the same buyer, and how hard do they compete on price vs. differentiation? (Braze, Iterable, Salesforce Marketing Cloud, moEngage)
2 Threat of New Entrants How easily can an AI-native startup with a thin wedge feature take a foothold? What's actually stopping them?
Center Your Product's Value Capture The margin, pricing power, and retention you actually get to keep — squeezed from all four sides
3 Buyer Power Can your enterprise customer credibly threaten to leave, multi-source, or beat your price down at renewal?
4 Supplier Power Can AWS, a deliverability network, or a foundation-model API raise your costs or degrade your product on a whim?
5 Threat of Substitutes Not a direct competitor — a different way to solve the same job. A marketing team stitching together Segment + a generic ESP + a BI dashboard instead of buying your platform. "Build it ourselves with an internal team + OpenAI's API" is a substitute, not a competitor.

The forces don't operate independently — they compound. A B2B platform facing low switching costs (weak rivalry defense) and a buyer base of sophisticated enterprise marketing teams (high buyer power) and a wave of AI-native entrants undercutting on price is in a structurally different position than one facing just a single pressure. Your job as PM isn't to memorize the five boxes — it's to ask, for any initiative: which force does this actually move, and by how much?

02 Worked Example 3 min

Should a B2B platform build its own AI content generator?

The Situation

A mid-market B2B SaaS product is deciding whether to build a native "AI drafts customer-facing content" feature in-house, versus partnering with an existing AI writing tool via API, versus doing nothing and letting customers use ChatGPT on the side.

Run it through the forces:

Rivalry — three direct competitors already shipped a version of this in the last two quarters. It's becoming table stakes, not a differentiator. Building it defends against losing deals, but doesn't win new ones.

New entrants — near zero. Any AI copywriting startup can wrap the same foundation model API in a weekend. This capability alone creates no barrier to entry for anyone.

Buyer power — customers increasingly expect this bundled free, not priced as an add-on. Asking them to pay separately invites exactly the price pushback that erodes margin.

Supplier power — building in-house means depending on a foundation-model vendor whose pricing and rate limits you don't control. That's a new supplier dependency, not a new moat.

Substitutes — customers already generate copy in ChatGPT and paste it in. The real substitute isn't a competitor's tool — it's the customer's own workflow outside your product entirely.

→

The senior PM read: build it — but budget it as a retention/parity cost, not a growth bet, and don't expect a price premium for it. The actual differentiation opportunity here isn't the copy generator itself; it's what only your platform has — behavioral and campaign performance data that no generic AI tool can see. That data-conditioning is the one thing rivals can't trivially copy. That's where the real moat question sits, and it's exactly what Day 7 is about.

04 Scenario Drill 8 min

Work through this before revealing the analysis

Your situation

You're the senior PM on a B2B SaaS product. Mid-market renewal rates have dropped six points over two quarters. Sales flags that a well-funded AI-native competitor is winning mid-market deals with a single pitch: "one-click automated workflows, at a third of your price, live in a week." Your platform is deeper and more configurable, but has a notoriously long implementation cycle for mid-market accounts. Your VP asks you to walk the exec team through this using Five Forces before proposing a response.

Before you reveal the analysis: which force(s) are actually shifting here — and is the right response a pricing move, a product move, or a segmentation move? Write down your own answer first.

Name the force precisely

This isn't really rivalry increasing in the abstract — it's a new entrant lowering the entry barrier that used to protect you (implementation complexity, integration depth) by targeting the segment where that barrier matters least. Mid-market buyers don't need your enterprise-grade configurability; they need speed. The entrant found the segment where your moat is actually a liability.

Resist the obvious move

The tempting response is a price cut — but that fights the entrant on the one dimension where they're structurally advantaged (lower cost base, no legacy enterprise architecture to maintain). Cutting price without cutting anything else just compresses your own margin while confirming their comparison holds.

The senior PM framing

This is a segmentation problem wearing a pricing costume. Your depth and configurability are real value — but only to accounts complex enough to need them. The fix isn't matching the entrant's speed platform-wide; it's building (or packaging) a fast, opinionated, lower-configuration onboarding path specifically for mid-market, so you're not asking a mid-market buyer to pay for enterprise depth they don't want. That directly targets the entry barrier the entrant exploited, without racing to the bottom on price for your core enterprise base.

What you'd bring to the exec team

"We're not losing on product quality — we're losing on time-to-value for a segment that doesn't need our depth. Recommend a lightweight mid-market onboarding tier, not a price cut, with a 90-day pilot before we consider any core pricing changes." That's a Five Forces read translated into a decision, not a framework recited for its own sake.

05 Meeting Move 2 min

Bring this to your next roadmap review

"Before we commit this — which of the five forces does it actually strengthen, and how long before a competitor can copy it in a single sprint?"

Use it when: a roadmap item is pitched purely on customer requests or competitive parity, without anyone naming which structural pressure it's meant to relieve. It forces the room to separate "customers will like this" from "this changes how much value we get to keep" — and it surfaces low-moat, easily-copied features before you spend a quarter building them.

06 Glossary 2 min

Say it like a FAANG PM

Value capture The share of the value a product creates that the company actually keeps as margin or pricing power, as opposed to value that leaks to customers, competitors, or suppliers.Distinct from "value creation" — a feature can create real value and still capture none of it.
Switching cost Everything a customer must give up or redo to leave your product for a competitor's — data migration, retraining, integration rework, contract penalties."Their switching cost is mostly the CRM workflow rebuild, not the data export."
Barrier to entry A structural reason a new competitor can't easily replicate your position — regulatory approval, data network effects, integration depth, capital requirements."Feature parity isn't a barrier to entry if it ships in a sprint."
Substitute A different category of solution to the same underlying job — not a rival product, but an alternative way to get the job done, including "do nothing" or "build it internally."Spreadsheets are a substitute for the platform, not a competing CRM.
Commoditization The process by which a once-differentiating feature becomes an expected baseline across a market, eliminating any price premium for having it."AI copy generation commoditized within two quarters — nobody pays extra for it now."
